What this collection covers

Sources, variants, and open edges

The 1995 anthology is indexed in full above. Below are 54 entries from papers, interviews, and recollections, followed by indexes of further numbered collections. Several wordings of one saying stay together. There’s no numerical limit on inclusion.

Quotation marks identify the words printed in the linked source. A report tells us how someone remembered or attributed a saying; it doesn’t establish a recording or its first use. Summaries carry a paraphrase label, with a route to the exact passage. Commentary is ours.

This is a documented collection in progress. Untranscribed recordings, inaccessible editions, translations, and private conversations leave gaps. The source record names the collections checked and the remaining leads; it doesn’t claim to include everything Heinz ever said.

01

Said to an observer

In his writing or an interview

‘Anything said is said to an observer.’

Von Foerster adds this to Humberto Maturana’s proposition, Anything said is said by an observer. The person speaking and the person addressed both belong in the account. What happens between them matters.

Heinz von Foerster, Cybernetics of Cybernetics (1979), pp. 5–8; first page of the linked PDF. He explicitly credits Maturana for the first proposition and presents the second as his own corollary.

The familiar wording

“Everything said is said to someone” is the formulation in the discussion that prompted this page. It’s a useful paraphrase; the quotation above retains the paper’s wording.

02

The ethical imperative

In his writing or an interview

‘Act always so as to increase the number of choices.’

Ask what your action makes possible, and for whom. My increased freedom may come at somebody else’s expense. Counting the options on a menu won’t settle that.

On Constructing a Reality (1973), final page. His later Ethics and Second-Order Cybernetics, pp. 288–289, distinguishes ethical commitments I make from moral commands imposed on others.

A first-person formulation

‘I shall act always so as to increase the total number of choices.’

This version makes the commitment personal. The circulating “Always increase the number of options” compresses the wording.

Disorder/Order: Discovery or Invention?. 1984, PDF p. 6, final paragraph before the discussion.

03

The aesthetical imperative

In his writing or an interview

‘If you desire to see, learn how to act.’

What we can perceive develops through what we do. Participate, vary the action, and notice what becomes available to observation.

On Constructing a Reality (1973), final page. This accompanies the ethical imperative in the same closing passage.

04

Meaning and the hearer

In his writing or an interview

‘The hearer, not the speaker determines the meaning of an utterance.’

Intending to communicate something doesn’t establish that it has been understood. Ask what the other person made of it. Their response changes what you can say next.

The 1995 anthology, item 4. This doesn’t release the speaker from responsibility for what they say.

05

Not seeing the absence

In his writing or an interview

‘One does not see what one does not see.’

A blind spot doesn’t announce itself as a hole. Another person, a changed position, or an unexpected result may expose something your account had no place for.

The 1995 anthology, item 5, on the blind spot.

06

Deciding the undecidable

In his writing or an interview

‘Only those questions that are in principle undecidable, we can decide.’

Where an agreed framework compels the answer, it has already settled the question. Where it cannot, we have to choose and take responsibility. Evidence still matters; it cannot relieve us of every judgement.

Ethics and Second-Order Cybernetics, in Understanding Understanding (2003), p. 293. First published in French in 1991; the English journal version appeared in 1992.

07

The missing observer

Reported or remembered

‘Objectivity is a subject’s delusion that observing can be done without him.’

The observer’s position, distinctions, and means of observing belong in the account. Acknowledging them creates obligations to explain and test a claim.

Bernhard Poerksen, The Certainty of Uncertainty, p. 3. Poerksen puts this attributed saying to von Foerster, who develops the argument in their conversation. This is the checked dialogue source, not a claim to have located its first use.

The 1984 essay also connects removing the observer from an account with avoiding responsibility. Read that argument, PDF p. 6.

08

Ignored problems, fame, and success

In his writing or an interview

‘The more profound the problem that is ignored, the greater are the chances for fame and success.’

A pointed question for any account of success: what had to be left outside it?

Responsibilities of Competence (1972), BCL #223, p. 1.

The Stafford Beer connection

Von Foerster says he presented the theorem at an earlier symposium and that Beer subsequently gave it its numbered name. The paper doesn’t establish a private exchange using the variant about ignoring more subjects and feigning success. That variant changes the wording and should be treated as a paraphrase.

The reported variant

“The more subjects you ignore, the greater your chance of feigning success” appears in Benjamin’s initiating post, attributed there to Heinz speaking to Beer. No primary passage for that wording has been located. It remains a reported variant.

09

Hard sciences, hard problems

Paraphrase — follow the original

The “hard” sciences succeed with comparatively tractable problems; the “soft” sciences face harder ones.

His second theorem challenges an easy ranking of disciplines. Simplifying a social problem may remove the interactions that matter.

Responsibilities of Competence. 1972, p. 1, Theorem Number Two. Paraphrase of his contrast; the exact formulation is in the paper.

10

Truth and the liar

In his writing or an interview

‘Truth is the invention of a liar.’

Follow this into the conversation about knowledge, certainty, and ethics. Using it as permission to disregard evidence would give the phrase rather too easy a life.

The title of Heinz von Foerster and Bernhard Poerksen’s book, subtitled Conversations on Epistemology and Ethics. The link is to the publisher’s record.

11

The biologist belongs in the theory

Paraphrase — follow the original

A biological account must also account for the living person who produces it.

This completes the paper’s three explicitly numbered theorems. A theory made by an organism must be able to accommodate that activity.

Responsibilities of Competence. The Single-Brain Problem, Theorem Number Three; reprinted on p. 195.

12

Reality and community

In his writing or an interview

‘Reality = Community’

The closing equation makes room for other people in the account of reality. It precedes the ethical and aesthetical imperatives.

On Constructing a Reality. Final PDF page 13, just before the two imperatives.

13

The perceived world as invention

Paraphrase — follow the original

The world we perceive is something we participate in producing.

This paraphrases the essay’s opening claim. The linked page preserves his precise formulation.

On Constructing a Reality. Opening sentence, PDF p. 1.

14

The language of order

In his writing or an interview

‘The length of descriptions is language-dependent.’

He links order and complexity to the descriptions an observer can produce. A changed descriptive language can change the apparent order.

Disorder/Order: Discovery or Invention?. PDF p. 5, concluding sequence of propositions.

15

Necessity and chance

Paraphrase — follow the original

Deductive certainty and inductive uncertainty concern our inference-making capacities.

His contrast places the observer’s abilities within the explanation of what appears necessary or accidental.

Disorder/Order: Discovery or Invention?. PDF p. 6, first paragraph; also anthology items 24–25.

16

Closed organisation, open exchanges

Paraphrase — follow the original

An organisation can maintain its own organisation while exchanging energy with its surroundings.

Von Foerster explicitly credits Maturana, Varela, and Uribe in this discussion. Closure in one respect needn’t mean isolation in every respect.

Disorder/Order: Discovery or Invention?. PDF p. 6, discussion of autopoiesis.

17

Learning to ask questions

In his writing or an interview

‘Education is learning to ask legitimate questions.’

Here the answer is not prescribed in advance. He credits Herbert Brün for this distinction and presents education as a necessity, beyond entitlement or privilege.

Perception of the Future and the Future of Perception. PDF p. 6, Future, propositions 1–2 and preceding paragraph. Address 1971; publication 1972.

18

Better off together

In his writing or an interview

‘A is better off when B is better off.’

The third proposition follows his account of education. Whose improvement is being pursued, and what happens to the other person?

Perception of the Future and the Future of Perception. PDF p. 6, Future, proposition 3.

19

Vision and light

In his writing or an interview

‘Let there be vision: and there was light.’

He closes by reversing the familiar order: what becomes visible depends on the capacity to see.

Perception of the Future and the Future of Perception. PDF p. 7, final sentence before the references.

Testing only for prescribed answers measures how predictable a student has become.

The paper questions making predictability an ideal for people simply because it is useful in manufactured devices.

Perception of the Future and the Future of Perception. PDF p. 6, educational trivialisation before Future.

Acting towards a future

Paraphrase — follow the original

An imagined future can orient present action; failing to act leaves others to act upon us.

In his writing or an interview

‘I am the observed relation between myself and observing myself.’

A formulation of self-reference: the description includes the act of describing oneself.

27

The whole and its parts

In his writing or an interview

‘the whole is neither more nor is it less than the sum of its parts: it is different.’

He is discussing the relationship between a recursive expression and the finite forms it entails. The difference matters more here than an arithmetic claim of superiority.

Objects: Tokens for (Eigen-)Behaviors. Printed p. 266; PDF p. 6, after expression (12). Excerpt from a longer sentence.

28

Objects and stable behaviour

In his writing or an interview

‘tokens for stable behaviors’

This is his phrase for objects in an account that includes the observer. Repeated sensorimotor coordination gives a route into the apparent stability of things.

Objects: Tokens for (Eigen-)Behaviors. Printed p. 261; PDF p. 1. Presented in 1976; reprinted in 2003.

29

Tell me your universe

In his writing or an interview

‘Tell me how the Universe came about, and I tell you who you are!’

Accounts of the world also disclose their speaker. The question invites attention to the person making the account.

Metaphysics of an Experimental Epistemologist. Metaphysics section, after the range of cultural accounts of origins.

30

Implicit ethics, explicit responsibility

In his writing or an interview

‘ethics becomes implicit, responsibility explicit.’

His choice of language is part of how ethics is practised. The observer’s participation can’t be handed off to an external authority.

Metaphysics of an Experimental Epistemologist. Closing section, paragraph about the collapse of hierarchy.

Expertise without disciplines

In his writing or an interview

‘I don't know where my expertise is; my expertise is no disciplines.’

Academic labels can restrict what someone is allowed to investigate.

‘Disciplines are an aftereffect of the institutional situation.’

Interview with Heinz von Foerster. Opening answer. Stefano Franchi, Güven Güzeldere, and Eric Minch; Stanford Humanities Review 4(2), 1995. Public transcript mirror.

Self-organisation needs an environment

In his writing or an interview

‘There are no such things as self-organizing systems!’

The provocation challenges a system treated independently of the environment that supports it. Read the qualification with the sentence.

On Self-Organizing Systems and Their Environments. Opening, printed p. 1; PDF p. 14. Essay published 1960, reprinted 2003.

People who knew him

Stories, remembered conversations, and a raccoon

Ernst von Glasersfeld · Written April 2000

An institute without administrators

‘A place where intelligent people can come and think, without being bothered by administrations.’

In Toledo, von Glasersfeld and Charlotte caught Heinz as he ran down a steep cobbled street, delighted at finding a possible home for their Institute of Free Research. They discussed it over lunch; the institute remained a dream.

Humberto Maturana · Written 29 April 2000

Chicho’s Promenade

‘he decided to call the path that I had opened with the tractor, Chicho's Promenade.’

During a 1978 visit to Rattlesnake Hill, Maturana lost control of a small tractor and rolled towards the house. He steered between the trees into bushes, stopping the tractor and leaving a new path. Heinz gave the accidental route a name.

Kevin Kreitman · Some Heinz Stories

Retirement, redefined

‘From now on, when I travel and speak and write, I will no longer be PAID for it!’

Kreitman remembers Heinz repeatedly promising to retire at 76. When his birthday arrived, a guest asked whether he really would. This was his explanation of what retirement meant.

Pille Bunnell · A visit in October 2000

What the raccoon made possible

‘the raccoon was a guide and an omen to the extent that I accepted it as such.’
