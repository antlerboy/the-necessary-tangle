Systems concepts: make your boundary inspectable

A first rehearsal of boundaries, relationships, perspectives, feedback and emergence. It is not a complete treatment of systems philosophy or complexity science.

Fictional case: Riverbank Library has a computer-booking service. Staff count a booking as successful when a slot is allocated. In one week 80 slots are allocated, 30 are not used, and 20 visitors leave without completing the online application they came to make. The booking form is online. A community group helps people who cannot use it; this work is absent from the library's statistics. Managers propose reducing staff assistance to allocate more slots. Staff predict that visitors will return repeatedly for help. Neither prediction has yet been tested.

  1. Draw an OU-style systems map for the booking manager's system of interest.

    Produce: A titled containment map with one stated purpose and boundary.

    One defensible title is 'Allocating computer slots'. Within its boundary place the booking process, slot records and allocation staff; put visitors, the community group and application websites in the environment. Containment means inclusion, not causation or seniority. This boundary can be useful for allocation work, but it excludes whether a visitor completes an application. Explain that exclusion rather than pretending the map covers the whole situation.

  2. Construct a second map from a visitor's purpose.

    Produce: A second boundary and a short explanation of what changed.

    A visitor may define the activity as 'Getting an application completed'. Include access to equipment, assistance, credentials, information and the application process. The library is now one contributor, not the centre of the universe. Include the community group's work. Different maps need not be merged into one allegedly objective map: their purposes differ.

  3. Write a testable feedback hypothesis about reduced assistance.

    Produce: One closed causal sentence and one observation that could challenge it.

    Less assistance may increase incomplete applications, which may increase repeat visits, which may consume assistance time and further reduce available assistance. This is a proposed reinforcing loop, not a fact established by the numbers. Compare repeat visits and assistance time before and during a small reversible change. A fall in repeats despite reduced assistance would challenge the proposed mechanism.

  4. Locate yourself in the inquiry.

    Produce: A two-sentence observer note.

    For example: 'I selected completed applications as important because I am taking the visitor's purpose seriously. My questions and reporting may make staff prioritise that measure, so I will ask who loses access or becomes invisible under this new boundary.' Naming the observer does not make every account equally adequate; each still needs evidence and a stated use.

Checks

  1. Does 80 allocated slots establish 80 successful visitor outcomes?

    • Yes: an output is an outcome
    • No: allocation and completion are different observations
    • No: all 80 bookings failed

    Answer: No: allocation and completion are different observations. Allocation is an output. The case does not establish 80 completed applications, nor does it establish that all bookings failed. The figures may overlap; do not subtract them as though they were disjoint categories.

  2. What does a boundary in an OU systems map primarily show?

    • A causal influence
    • An editorial inclusion in a stated system of interest
    • A manager's authority

    Answer: An editorial inclusion in a stated system of interest. Containment identifies inclusion at the chosen level. Causal influence and authority require other representations or explicit additional notation.

  3. Which observation supports emergence without claiming magic?

    • Repeated local booking decisions can generate an access pattern nobody intended
    • Every large organisation is unpredictable in every respect
    • A whole has no relationship to its parts

    Answer: Repeated local booking decisions can generate an access pattern nobody intended. An aggregate pattern can arise through interactions rather than anyone designing it. That does not imply universal unpredictability or the irrelevance of components.

Repair

The map places every organisation in one circle labelled 'the whole system' and claims that everyone shares the library's success measure.

Give the map a purpose, author, boundary and date. Distinguish an allocation perspective from an application-completion perspective. Record disagreement and missing information. 'Whole' is a claim to examine, not a label that removes the boundary problem.

Changed case

A new manager counts only applications completed on library computers. What exclusion could this introduce, and how would you test it?

People who complete applications later at home or with the community group disappear from the outcome measure. So may people prevented from attending. Ask a sample of visitors about later completion and contact the community group with permission. Do not solve the exclusion merely by broadening a diagram without changing how evidence is collected.

Review criteria

  • Each map has a purpose and an explicit boundary.
  • Containment and causal links are not conflated.
  • Observed facts, interpretations and predictions are distinguished.
  • The observer's choices and an excluded perspective are named.

Systems laws: test variety, feedback and local optimisation

Basic examples of requisite variety, feedback delay and whole-system effects. The SCiO row is broader; this page does not claim to cover every law in a systems-laws course.

Fictional case: a help desk receives four mutually exclusive request states: A password reset, B identity discrepancy, C accessibility support and D suspected fraud. Its safe responses are R1 an automated reset for A, R2 a trained identity investigation for B, R3 supported access for C and R4 a fraud investigation for D. Applying any other response to a state leaves an unacceptable outcome in this deliberately simplified exercise. The new portal can recognise only 'ordinary' and 'exceptional', and offers only R1 or 'send an email'. Replies to that email take five working days. Managers propose counting cases closed by the portal as success.

  1. Construct the disturbance-response table.

    Produce: Four rows showing the necessary distinction, safe response and current gap.

    A requires R1 and is handled. B requires R2, C requires R3 and D requires R4; the portal's email route does not itself supply those responses. Four labels alone do not solve the problem: recognition, authority, capability and timeliness must support the matching action. In this toy case the safe-response sets are disjoint, so a regulator restricted to two effective responses cannot meet all four requirements.

  2. Design one legitimate attenuation and one amplification.

    Produce: Two changes, each explaining what variety is changed.

    An authorised upstream identity correction could prevent some B discrepancies arising: it changes the disturbance reaching the desk. Training and authorising an adviser to distinguish B, C and D and route them immediately increases effective response variety. Hiding fraud or accessibility requests from the report merely suppresses information; it does not attenuate the underlying need safely.

  3. Test the five-day feedback delay.

    Produce: A failure mechanism and an observable warning signal.

    People may resubmit while waiting, increasing workload before the original exception is resolved. Track unique people, repeated submissions, waiting age and unresolved risk, not just portal closures. A lagged weekly total can look reassuring while urgent exceptions accumulate. More frequent reporting helps only if someone can act on the signal.

  4. Replace the local success claim with a test of the whole service.

    Produce: One outcome measure and one counter-measure.

    Measure safe resolution of the person's request end to end, with unresolved age and access exclusions as counter-measures. Portal closures may be retained as an operational count, but cannot substitute for resolution. The chosen definition of 'safe' and who can contest it must remain explicit.

Checks

  1. Under this case's disjoint-response assumption, how many distinguishable effective responses are required?

    • Two, because there are two portal labels
    • Four
    • An unlimited number

    Answer: Four. Four states require four different safe responses in this constructed model. This is not a universal rule that four categories always require four employees or channels.

  2. Which proposal changes the disturbance rather than merely hiding it?

    • Exclude inaccessible requests from the dashboard
    • Correct a recurring upstream identity-record defect
    • Rename all exceptions 'ordinary'

    Answer: Correct a recurring upstream identity-record defect. Correcting the defect can reduce actual discrepancies. Exclusion and relabelling alter the representation while leaving the need or risk.

  3. Can a daily dashboard compensate for an adviser with no authority to act?

    • Yes, information alone supplies control
    • No, sensing and effective action are both needed
    • Yes, provided it contains more indicators

    Answer: No, sensing and effective action are both needed. Observation without an available, timely response does not regulate the disturbance. More indicators may instead overload the recipient.

Repair

'The law of requisite variety proves that every customer needs a unique process and standardisation is always wrong.'

The law concerns necessary distinctions and effective regulation relative to desired outcomes. Several disturbances may share an adequate response. Standardise where differences do not matter to that outcome; preserve distinctions and discretion where they do. State the equivalence assumption and test it.

Changed case

B and D can now be safely handled by the same authorised investigation team using one triage action. Does the four-response conclusion still follow?

No. The original disjoint-response assumption has changed. Three initial response classes may suffice if the investigation route actually distinguishes and handles the later differences in time. Count effective response capability across the relevant process, not merely the first screen's buttons.

Review criteria

  • The essential outcome and unacceptable states are explicit.
  • Response capability is not reduced to headcount or number of labels.
  • Attenuating a disturbance is distinguished from suppressing its signal.
  • A claim is revised when its modelling assumptions change.

Transposing theory: move up to a pattern and back down

A basic abstraction-and-return rehearsal for the framework's transposing-theory row. Its resource list also cites Yi Lin's systemic yoyo; this exercise does not teach or validate that distinct formal model. The terminology and intended depth should be checked with your tutor.

Fictional case A: a warehouse orders replacement stock from last month's sales. Delivery takes four weeks. When shelves empty, several managers independently raise urgent orders. Stock later arrives together and exceeds storage capacity. Fictional case B: a council trains additional caseworkers using last quarter's backlog. Training takes six months. Several services independently commission the same small pool of trainers. Trainees arrive after demand has changed. Casework, unlike boxes, also varies in urgency, consent, relationship and complexity.

  1. Write the common structural pattern without using warehouse or council nouns.

    Produce: A four-part abstract model.

    A demand signal is delayed; a replenishment decision is made from it; the replenishment takes time; independent decision-makers may duplicate orders while the pipeline is invisible. The resulting overshoot is a hypothesis generated by that structure. 'Both organisations need better leadership' is too unspecific to transpose or test.

  2. Map the abstract elements into case B.

    Produce: A correspondence table with at least one non-correspondence.

    Stock corresponds only partially to available trained capacity; orders correspond to training commissions; delivery lead time to training delay; hidden pipeline to trainees already commissioned. People are not interchangeable inventory. Capabilities, supervision, retention, consent and case mix alter the comparison. State where the analogy stops.

  3. Derive a modest intervention and a falsification test.

    Produce: One intervention hypothesis with a measurable challenge.

    Make current training commitments and expected completion dates visible across services before commissioning more. Predict fewer duplicated commissions without reducing necessary capability. Compare unique requirements, commissions and later staffing gaps. If duplication persists because services require incompatible skills, the shared-inventory model needs revision rather than stronger enforcement.

Checks

  1. Which is the transferable part of the example?

    • A warehouse's staffing ratio
    • The delayed signal, replenishment pipeline and duplicated decisions
    • The claim that people are stock

    Answer: The delayed signal, replenishment pipeline and duplicated decisions. The proposed correspondence concerns relationships and delays. Neither an arbitrary parameter nor a literal identity between people and inventory follows.

  2. What should happen when case mix invalidates interchangeability?

    • Discard the evidence to protect the analogy
    • Revise the abstraction or stop using it
    • Assume the model applies because it is systemic

    Answer: Revise the abstraction or stop using it. A theory is useful through an explicit fit with the situation. A failed correspondence is information about its limit.

Repair

'Hospitals are factories, so the warehouse rule should be applied unchanged to patients.'

Name the limited structural correspondence, then examine consent, urgency, variable needs, clinical judgement and care relationships. Transfer a question or tested mechanism, not a whole identity or managerial prescription.

Changed case

Apply the same pattern to recruiting volunteer interpreters. Name one correspondence and one reason a numerical inventory rule might fail.

A recruitment-to-availability delay is a correspondence. Language combinations, availability, confidentiality and matching requirements make volunteer capacity heterogeneous. A single total may hide a shortage in one language alongside unused capacity in another.

Review criteria

  • The abstract pattern contains relationships, not just similar vocabulary.
  • Correspondences and non-correspondences are both recorded.
  • A practical prediction could be challenged by evidence.
  • The learner returns to and revises the original situation.

Soft Systems Methodology: build a model that follows its purpose

A compact SSM rehearsal. The stages organise learning here; they are not a claim that SSM is a mandatory linear project sequence. An activity model is an inquiry device, not a description of what the organisation really is.

Fictional case: a repair cooperative allocates home-repair visits. Its scheduler wants every technician hour booked. Residents want a usable home and predictable appointments. Technicians want the information and parts needed to finish safely. The funder pays for completed visits, even when another visit is needed. Residents with uncertain work shifts often miss appointments. The cooperative's board can change booking rules but cannot compel a resident to be at home. There is no evidence yet about which change would improve completed repairs.

  1. Make a rich picture of the situation.

    Produce: One picture or accessible narrative showing the competing concerns.

    Include residents, technicians, scheduler, funder and board; show funding for visits, information and parts, uncertain shifts, missed appointments and conflicting meanings of success. Show tensions and absences, not only a flowchart. A resident's absence is an event to understand, not proof of irresponsibility.

  2. Write two relevant root definitions using PQR.

    Produce: Two distinct purposeful perspectives, each one sentence.

    Resident perspective: 'Enable agreed safe repairs to be completed, by coordinating access, diagnosis, parts and work with residents, in order to restore a usable home with manageable disruption.' Scheduling perspective: 'Allocate available technician time, by matching eligible jobs to slots and skills, in order to use the cooperative's funded capacity.' Neither is declared the true purpose. The difference is useful for inquiry.

  3. Check the resident definition with CATWOE.

    Produce: Six explicit entries, including the transformation.

    Customers: residents benefiting or suffering, including those excluded by booking rules. Actors: residents, booking staff, technicians and suppliers carrying out the activities. Transformation: an agreed repair need becomes a completed, checked repair, not simply a person becoming 'a customer'. Weltanschauung: restoring a usable home justifies organising around completion and disruption. Owner: the board can stop or change the cooperative's arrangement; residents also control access. Environment: safety obligations, available skills, supply times and constraints outside this model's control. Do not put the proposed new booking rule in Environment merely because it exists today.

  4. Build the minimum activity model for the resident definition.

    Produce: Five to nine verb-led activities with dependencies and monitoring.

    A defensible model is: agree the repair need; establish access constraints; diagnose work and materials; arrange a feasible visit; perform the repair; check completion with the resident; monitor performance and revise arrangements. 'Arrange a feasible visit' depends on access and diagnosis; performance review receives evidence from completion checking. Assess efficacy (did the repair occur?), efficiency (what resources and effort did it use?) and effectiveness (does it contribute to the longer-term purpose?). These are logical requirements of this definition, not a copy of departments.

  5. Compare the model with the case and propose one accommodation.

    Produce: A three-row comparison and a feasible next inquiry.

    For 'check completion', the case reports paid visits but not resident-verified completion: investigate the gap. For 'establish access', uncertain shifts are known but the booking response is not: ask residents. For 'monitor', the funder's measure may conflict with completion: discuss a paired completion measure. A possible accommodation is to retain visit reporting while jointly trialling resident-confirmed completion and flexible scheduling. It needs consent and negotiation; a neat model does not establish agreement.

Checks

  1. Which transformation matches the resident root definition?

    • Unallocated slots become allocated slots
    • Agreed repair needs become completed, checked repairs
    • Residents become compliant

    Answer: Agreed repair needs become completed, checked repairs. The transformation must follow the selected purpose. Slot allocation belongs to the other root definition; compliance changes the purpose without acknowledging it.

  2. What is wrong with activity boxes labelled Board, Finance and Technician?

    • They are roles or units, not logically required activities
    • They are always outside the system
    • Nothing: any organisation chart is an activity model

    Answer: They are roles or units, not logically required activities. Use verbs describing necessary activities. Roles may perform them, but a departmental chart does not establish the activity logic.

  3. A model differs from current practice. What follows?

    • Current practice must immediately copy the model
    • The difference supplies a question for structured comparison
    • The model is necessarily false

    Answer: The difference supplies a question for structured comparison. SSM uses the comparison for learning and debate. The model is purposeful and selective; the difference does not by itself command a redesign or invalidate the inquiry.

Repair

Root definition: restore usable homes. Model: maximise visits, close tickets, penalise missed appointments. No completion check.

The model has silently substituted the funder's or scheduler's purpose. Either label it as another purposeful model or rebuild it around agreed needs, completion and resident experience. Add monitoring that can challenge the arrangement, not merely count its transactions.

Changed case

The board now insists on minimising disruption rather than maximising technician utilisation. Add one necessary activity and one comparison question.

An explicit activity could be 'agree acceptable timing and disruption with the resident'. Ask whether current booking captures and honours those constraints. Other activities are acceptable when logically derived and not simply desirable extras. Record the unresolved trade-off with capacity rather than assuming it disappears.

Review criteria

  • At least two purposes remain distinguishable.
  • CATWOE supports rather than replaces a coherent root definition.
  • Activities logically follow the selected purpose and include monitoring.
  • Comparison produces inquiry and possible accommodation, not a claim that the model is reality.

System Dynamics: build, calculate and challenge a queue model

An original discrete-time stock-flow rehearsal with a deliberately simple boundary. It is not a prediction of a real service and does not validate a model merely because it runs.

Fictional case: a service starts week 1 with a backlog of 40 cases. Exactly 12 new cases arrive at the start of each week. Staff can complete at most 10 cases per week in weeks 1 and 2. Additional trained capacity is available from the start of week 3, raising the limit to 16. All cases are identical in this toy model. Completions during a week cannot exceed that week's starting backlog plus arrivals. There are no withdrawals or reopens in the first model.

  1. Draw the stock-flow structure and state its units.

    Produce: One stock, two flows and an explicit time step.

    Stock: backlog in cases. Inflow: arrivals in cases/week. Outflow: completions in cases/week. Capacity is a limit on the outflow, not another stock of cases. For a one-week step: end backlog = start backlog + arrivals - completions; completions = min(capacity, start backlog + arrivals). The minimum prevents negative backlog. Changing the time step would require rates and event timing to be handled consistently.

  2. Calculate the first four end-of-week backlogs.

    Produce: A four-row calculation table.

    Week 1: 40 + 12 - 10 = 42. Week 2: 42 + 12 - 10 = 44. Week 3: 44 + 12 - 16 = 40. Week 4: 40 + 12 - 16 = 36. Backlog rises before the extra capacity arrives, then falls by four cases per week while enough cases remain. A drop in arrivals is not needed to explain this change.

  3. Describe a balancing feedback that could generate the capacity decision.

    Produce: A signed loop and its delay.

    Backlog (+) increases perceived capacity gap; gap (+) increases training commissions; commissions (+), after a training delay, increase effective completion capacity; capacity (+) increases completions; completions (-) reduce backlog. The single negative causal link makes this proposed loop balancing. The fixed capacity schedule in the numerical exercise is externally specified: the loop is not yet implemented in its equations. Do not claim a two-flow stock model already simulates the decision feedback.

  4. Run two extreme-condition checks.

    Produce: Results for zero arrivals and for an almost empty queue.

    With zero arrivals and backlog 40, capacity 10 yields backlog 30 after one week. With starting backlog 2, arrivals 0 and capacity 16, completions are 2 and ending backlog 0, not -14. These are necessary accounting checks, not evidence that the real service behaves like the model.

  5. Identify a missing mechanism that could reverse the policy conclusion.

    Produce: One boundary extension and data needed to assess it.

    Rushed work might reopen cases, making nominal completions overstate resolved need. Add a reopen inflow with an explicit timing rule and measure its rate. Alternatively, training may temporarily reduce experienced staff availability. A model boundary that omits these effects may overstate the improvement from additional capacity.

Checks

  1. What is the backlog at the end of week 2?

    • 20 cases
    • 44 cases
    • 64 cases

    Answer: 44 cases. The backlog gains 12-10=2 cases in each of two weeks: 40+2+2=44. Capacity is a rate of completion, not the number left waiting.

  2. Which unit belongs to the backlog?

    • Cases/week
    • Cases
    • Weeks/case

    Answer: Cases. Backlog accumulates cases. A flow has cases/week, and a processing time may have weeks/case under a specified interpretation.

  3. Does a positive link mean a beneficial effect?

    • Yes
    • No: it means change in the same direction, other influences held constant
    • Only for reinforcing loops

    Answer: No: it means change in the same direction, other influences held constant. Polarity describes a causal direction, not desirability. A reinforcing loop can amplify either improvement or deterioration.

  4. Starting backlog 2, zero arrivals, capacity 16: how many cases complete?

    • 16
    • 2
    • -14

    Answer: 2. The outflow is limited by available cases. A negative backlog is an accounting defect, not an innovative service model.

Repair

Backlog = arrivals / completions. It is labelled 'cases', and managers say its fall proves a balancing feedback loop.

The ratio of two rates is dimensionless and is not an accumulation. Use the stock accounting equation with an initial condition. A changing ratio does not identify a closed causal mechanism. State and test the decision rules and delays separately.

Changed case

From week 3, let 25% of each week's 16 completions return as new backlog at the end of that same week. Calculate end backlogs for weeks 3 and 4.

Each week four cases reopen. Week 3 ends at 44+12-16+4=44; week 4 also ends at 44. Gross completion capacity increased but net resolution is only 12 cases/week. This timing is stipulated for the exercise; a real reopen delay must be investigated rather than assumed.

Review criteria

  • Stocks, rates, units, initial conditions and timing are explicit.
  • The four baseline calculations and empty-queue test are correct.
  • Loop polarity is causal rather than moral.
  • The numerical model is distinguished from an unimplemented feedback hypothesis.
  • A missing mechanism and a way to test it are named.

Viable System Model: diagnose functions and channels

A basic organisational diagnosis. Functions can be distributed or combined in people and groups. The exercise does not establish that every multi-agency network has a single legitimate metasystem.

Fictional case: Neighbourhood Advice Cooperative runs North, Central and South advice centres. Each serves local residents, organises its own advisers and decides ordinary cases. They share specialist interpreters and sometimes double-book them. A cooperative operations group negotiates budgets and capacity with centre leads. A quality reviewer periodically observes cases directly, with an agreed learning remit. A small planning group studies changing resident needs and technology, but its reports rarely reach operations. The elected board sets identity and purpose and settles major policy disputes. An urgent safeguarding concern currently travels through three monthly reporting meetings.

  1. State the focal system and one level above and below it.

    Produce: A recursion statement and three candidate operational units.

    Focal system: the advice cooperative as a whole. Candidate System 1 units: the three centres, each with an operation, local management and a relevant environment. Within a centre, case teams may form the next level if they genuinely carry primary activity with appropriate management. A wider partnership is only a candidate level above: do not assert that it governs the cooperative without evidence and legitimacy.

  2. Map the case evidence to Systems 1-5 and 3*.

    Produce: A function-evidence table, leaving unknowns visible.

    System 1: centres delivering advice in their local environments. System 2: arrangements damping interference between centres, such as interpreter booking protocols; the double-booking suggests a gap. System 3: the operations group's resource bargains, synergy and internal regulation. System 3*: the agreed direct sampling of operational reality, distinct from routine reports. System 4: the planning group's outside-and-future intelligence. System 5: identity, policy and balancing present and future through the elected board. These are functions evidenced by activities, not job titles.

  3. Diagnose two channel failures.

    Produce: Two hypotheses, each with a verification question.

    The System 3/4 dialogue may be weak: ask how a recent external change altered an operational decision, and how operational experience revised a future proposal. Urgent safeguarding signals are too slow for their purpose: trace a concern's actual escalation and response time. A System 2 booking weakness is another defensible hypothesis. A missing line in your diagram alone is not proof that the function is absent.

  4. Design a correction that preserves local autonomy.

    Produce: One bounded change and a check for over-centralisation.

    Give centres a shared interpreter availability view and agreed conflict-resolution protocol, while leaving normal case decisions local. Establish a direct urgent escalation route with named response responsibility. For future adaptation, create a recurring 3/4 conversation using both demand evidence and operational constraints. Check whether the new arrangement removes local discretion that does not need to be centralised.

  5. Test the diagnosis with operational evidence.

    Produce: A short sampling plan.

    Trace an ordinary case, a cross-centre resource conflict, a future-driven change and an urgent exception. Ask the people doing the work how information and authority actually move. Distinguish formal policy from observed practice. Use direct observation with a legitimate remit, not covert inspection presented as neutral learning.

Checks

  1. Which is the strongest System 2 example in this case?

    • A shared interpreter scheduling protocol
    • The elected board's identity statement
    • A future-demand study

    Answer: A shared interpreter scheduling protocol. The protocol reduces interference among operational units. Identity is a System 5 concern; outside-and-future intelligence is System 4.

  2. What distinguishes System 3* from routine System 3 reports?

    • It is a more senior manager
    • It provides a direct, agreed check on operational reality
    • It is every financial audit, regardless of purpose

    Answer: It provides a direct, agreed check on operational reality. The distinction is the information channel and function, not rank or the word 'audit'. Its remit and effect on trust still matter.

  3. Must the three centres have identical local arrangements to be System 1 units?

    • Yes
    • No, they need viable local responses within the cooperative's identity and constraints
    • Only if the chart is symmetrical

    Answer: No, they need viable local responses within the cooperative's identity and constraints. Different environments may require different response variety. Coherence does not require identical local operations.

Repair

The chief executive is labelled S5, all middle managers S2, every worker S1, and the outside world is omitted.

Choose the recursion and primary activities first. Identify actual functions, environments and channels. One person may perform several functions; a worker may exercise local intelligence and policy. An organisational rank is not a VSM system number.

Changed case

An autonomous helpline is added with its own users, case decisions and local management. Is it necessarily System 2 because it connects centres?

No. It may be another System 1 unit if advice delivery is a primary activity at the chosen recursion. Some of its activities could also support coordination. Determine function, operational autonomy and environment rather than classifying it by the word 'helpline'.

Review criteria

  • The recursion and system of interest are explicit.
  • Operational units include operation, management and environment.
  • Systems 2, 3, 3*, 4 and 5 are differentiated by function.
  • The diagnosis tests channels and evidence, not just missing boxes.
  • Corrections consider autonomy, legitimacy and timely response.

Critical Systems Heuristics: inspect all twelve boundary categories

This exercise checks the completeness and consistency of boundary reasoning. It does not supply a morally unique answer or pretend that the practitioner represents every affected person.

Fictional case: a council proposes replacing its assisted benefits-application desk with a digital service. The finance director sponsors the proposal and counts annual administrative cost as success. A vendor controls the software design. The council controls staffing and opening hours. An analyst advises using completion rates from current online users. People unable to start online are absent from that dataset. A tenants' group has asked to be heard but has no place in the decision meetings. The project document says 'digital by default is modern and fair'. No replacement assistance or appeal route has been agreed.

  1. Complete the four groups of boundary categories in the descriptive 'is' mode.

    Produce: Twelve labelled entries, separating case evidence from unknowns.

    Motivation: beneficiary, purpose, measure. The sponsor prioritises administrative savings; whether all applicants benefit is unestablished. Control: decision-maker, controlled resources, decision environment. Council staffing decisions and vendor software control differ; applicants' internet access is not simply a project-controlled resource. Knowledge: expert, relevant expertise, guarantor of success. Analyst/vendor knowledge dominates; current online completion is treated as assurance, but excludes non-starters. Legitimacy: witness for affected people, emancipation or opportunity to challenge, and worldview. The tenants' group is not yet represented; an appeal route is absent; 'modern and fair' is a contested framing. Use 'unknown' where the case does not specify a fact.

  2. Write an 'ought' counterpart for each category.

    Produce: Twelve proposals with the person or group whose judgement they express.

    One defensible account includes all eligible applicants as intended beneficiaries; successful access to entitlement as purpose; cost, completion, exclusion and effort as measures; explicit council accountability and vendor constraints; assisted access resources; knowledge from applicants and frontline staff; assurance through disaggregated evidence and challenge; an independent voice for affected non-participants; accessible contest and redress; and a worldview in which access is not conditional on digital confidence. These are proposals to discuss, not automatically legitimate claims made on others' behalf.

  3. Trace one boundary change into the evaluation.

    Produce: A before-and-after judgement with a changed evidence requirement.

    If the beneficiary boundary expands from current online users to all eligible applicants, the online completion rate is no longer sufficient. Count failed starts and people using or needing assistance, and ask about effort and exclusion. An apparently cheaper process may transfer cost or prevent access. The new judgement requires evidence; it is not proof that digital service is necessarily bad.

  4. Design a challenge that an affected person can actually use.

    Produce: One route to contest the proposal without accepting its framing.

    Invite independently supported evidence from people unable to use the digital route, permit oral or assisted submissions, explain how challenges affect the decision, and provide a route to disagree after a meeting. Do not require a person to complete an online consultation to prove that online access is a barrier. Representation and practical ability to challenge are different questions.

Checks

  1. Does the analyst's involvement resolve the legitimacy questions?

    • Yes: expertise represents everyone
    • No: expertise and the voice of affected people are different categories
    • Yes: the data are numerical

    Answer: No: expertise and the voice of affected people are different categories. Expertise can inform an inquiry without supplying a witness for affected people or the practical freedom to challenge its assumptions.

  2. What does 'ought' mean in this exercise?

    • A hidden fact waiting to be discovered
    • An explicit normative boundary proposal open to challenge
    • The tutor's mandatory policy preference

    Answer: An explicit normative boundary proposal open to challenge. The task is to make values and boundary choices discussable. A model answer illustrates reasoning, not an authorised settlement of people's interests.

  3. Who is missing from the stated online completion measure?

    • Only the vendor
    • People unable to start the online process

Repair

A stakeholder list has twelve names and is labelled 'the twelve CSH questions answered'.

People are not boundary categories. Examine motivation, control, knowledge and legitimacy in both descriptive and normative modes. A single actor may occupy several roles; a crucial role may have no credible representative.

Changed case

The tenants' group gets one seat but cannot alter the agenda or challenge the savings target. Which part of the critique remains open?

  • All twelve categories are addressed in both is and ought modes.
  • Evidence, absence and normative proposals are distinguishable.

Confrontation Analysis: separate positions, options and credibility

An introductory confrontation-analysis rehearsal, not a full formal classification of dilemmas or a prediction of behaviour. Consent, new options and changing preferences remain possible.

Fictional case: a council and a charity are negotiating an evening advice service. The council alone can authorise funding (F). The charity alone can commit a trained team (T). Both publicly propose F=yes and T=yes. The council says it will withhold funding if the charity does not commit. The charity says it will not commit without funding. Both prefer a funded staffed service to no service. The charity doubts that the council's promised funding will survive its next budget meeting. A council manager's personal assurance is not a budget authorisation. The charity could offer an unpaid four-week pilot, but has not offered or agreed to do so.

  1. List actors, controlled options and the four possible combinations.

    Produce: An options table with controllers clearly marked.

    F is controlled by the council and T by the charity. Combinations are (yes,yes), (yes,no), (no,yes), and (no,no). Each actor's stated position is (yes,yes). Stated fallbacks under the other's refusal lead to (no,no). Do not enter the unpaid pilot as a commitment: it is only a potential new option.

  2. Separate preference, intention and belief.

    Produce: Three statements drawn from the case, each correctly typed.

    Preference: both prefer the staffed funded service to none. Announced intention: each makes its contribution conditional on the other. Belief: the charity doubts future council funding. These are not interchangeable. Agreement on a preferred outcome does not resolve distrust about whether the other party will perform.

  3. Propose a change that addresses credibility without coercion.

    Produce: One revised option and evidence that it is genuinely available.

    The council could seek formal budget authorisation and offer an agreement with a staged payment before staff mobilisation. The charity could then make a bounded staffing commitment conditional on that authorisation. Verify decision rights, dates and resources. A louder promise does not change the relevant evidence; neither does pressuring volunteers into an unauthorised pilot.

  4. Rebuild the table after the change.

    Produce: A revised commitment record and one remaining uncertainty.

    Replace the vague F option with 'funding formally authorised and first payment made by date X', if the council can actually do it. Make the charity's staffing obligation conditional on that observable event. Remaining uncertainty may include recruitment, payment timing or future continuation. State who can withdraw, how notice works and what happens to users if the service stops.

Checks

  1. Who controls the charity's staffing commitment?

    • The council, because it funds the service
    • The charity
    • The analyst drawing the table

    Answer: The charity. Influence is not unilateral control. The charity's authorised decision-makers control its commitment in this case.

  2. Does a shared preferred outcome prove cooperation will occur?

    • Yes
    • No, beliefs about performance and credible commitments still matter
    • Only if an analyst assigns utility scores

    Answer: No, beliefs about performance and credible commitments still matter. Preference, announced intention and credibility differ. Inventing numbers does not resolve missing commitment evidence.

  3. How should the possible unpaid pilot appear?

    • As an agreed charity commitment
    • As an uncommitted potential new option
    • As proof that funding is unnecessary

    Answer: As an uncommitted potential new option. The scenario says it has not been offered. Treating possibility as agreement would distort the confrontation and the charity's position.

Repair

The options table assigns 'charity supplies staff' to the council and treats a manager's assurance as secured funding.

Correct the option controller and distinguish authority, resources and announcements. Record the charity's doubt without asserting it is factually correct. Seek evidence and redesign commitments rather than diagnosing personalities.

Changed case

The council obtains authorised funding, but the charity now doubts it can recruit enough qualified staff. What changes in the analysis?

The original funding credibility issue may be reduced, while the charity's capability to fulfil its promise becomes uncertain. Introduce a feasible staged staffing option, recruitment evidence or a revised start date. Do not continue treating every obstacle as the council's failure.

Review criteria

  • Each option has its actual controller.
  • Positions, fallbacks, preferences and beliefs remain distinct.
  • The proposed change affects a specific credibility or feasibility issue.
  • New options and revised preferences are allowed without inventing consent.

INFORMED Group Dynamics: investigate the distinctions behind a decision

An original introductory rehearsal using the publicly described INFORMED framing and personal-construct orientation. It is not Marguet's complete taught protocol, a personality assessment, or a validated repertory-grid diagnosis. Use supervised teaching for the fuller approach.

Fictional case: three managers are choosing how to improve an advice service. Aisha supports a shared portal because residents should not have to repeat their story. Ben supports named advisers because difficult cases need continuity. Cara supports walk-in access because a portal-only service could exclude people. A meeting note labels Aisha 'efficient', Ben 'resistant' and Cara 'emotional'. No manager has agreed with those labels. Options are P: portal only; N: named adviser with telephone access; H: a hybrid service with named continuity and assisted digital access.

  1. Investigate narratives before interpreting motives.

    Produce: One neutral follow-up question for each manager.

    Ask Aisha for an example of harmful repetition, Ben for an example where continuity changed a result, and Cara for an example of exclusion. Ask what would count as an improvement in each story. Do not ask 'Why are you resistant?' because that imports the meeting note's untested explanation.

  2. Frame the orientations as provisional contrasting constructs.

    Produce: Three distinctions, with a note that the speaker must validate them.

    Possible contrasts are 'tell the story once / repeatedly start again', 'continuity / fragmented hand-offs', and 'accessible assistance / exclusion at entry'. These are analyst proposals based on the supplied statements, not constructs actually elicited from the people. Avoid replacing them with generic categories such as 'pro-change / anti-change'.

  3. Make a small reflective option map.

    Produce: A matrix of P, N and H against the three contrasts, retaining unknowns.

    A plausible first map marks P as potentially reducing repetition but uncertain on continuity and poor on assisted access; N as potentially supporting continuity and telephone access, with repetition across other services unknown; H as potentially supporting all three, subject to design and resources. These are hypotheses. No supplied evidence justifies precise scores or the conclusion that H is automatically best.

  4. Explore distinctions and revise your first story.

    Produce: A revised account and a question that could expose a real disagreement.

    The managers may all seek better access but distinguish its conditions differently. Ask what each would preserve or refuse in H, including costs, privacy and staffing. Genuine conflict may remain. The exercise succeeds when it improves inquiry, not when it manufactures consensus or claims to read minds.

Checks

  1. What is the status of 'Ben is resistant'?

    • An observed fact
    • An interpretation not validated by Ben
    • A required INFORMED category

    Answer: An interpretation not validated by Ben. The observation is his stated preference for continuity. 'Resistant' adds a motive or judgement the case does not establish.

  2. Who should validate a proposed personal construct?

    • Only the analyst
    • The person whose meaning is being represented
    • The highest-ranking manager

    Answer: The person whose meaning is being represented. An analyst can suggest a contrast, but should not replace the person's account with an imposed category.

  3. Can the unscored map establish that H is optimal?

    • Yes, because it sounds inclusive
    • No: feasibility, evidence and people's judgements remain to be examined
    • Yes, if it is drawn neatly

    Answer: No: feasibility, evidence and people's judgements remain to be examined. A representation surfaces questions. It is not a substitute for evidence or agreement on criteria.

Repair

The facilitator invents three personality scores and uses them to explain why one manager must be excluded from the decision.

Return to statements, stories and participant-validated distinctions. Keep tentative inferences visible and invite correction. This rehearsal does not authorise psychometric claims or exclusion based on inferred motives.

Changed case

Ben now says he supports the portal provided a resident can retain a named adviser. Revise the account.

His objection was not necessarily to technology. Continuity may be the relevant distinction. Update the option map and ask how the portal would preserve it. Retain any new concerns rather than declaring the disagreement permanently resolved.

Review criteria

  • Narratives are sought before motives are assigned.
  • Contrasting constructs remain provisional until checked with their owners.
  • Unknowns are not converted into invented numeric scores.
  • The map changes at least one inquiry or interpretation.

Interactive Management: construct a small interpretive structural model

This rehearses one modelling component of Interactive Management. It does not reproduce its full facilitated group process or establish group agreement. The external handbook's free-download availability is uncertain, so the complete case is supplied here.

Fictional case: a partnership is opening a shared referral service. Five conditions are proposed: A agreed decision authority; B permission to access required records; C a workable triage protocol; D an allocation process; E a follow-up review. For this exercise the group has explicitly agreed these immediate prerequisites: A before C, B before C, C before D, and D before E. There are no other immediate prerequisite links. The relation being modelled is 'must be in place before', not 'causes better outcomes'.

  1. Create the immediate-link matrix in order A, B, C, D, E.

    Produce: Five rows of five binary values, with a legend.

    With rows as prerequisites and columns as dependent conditions, and zero diagonal for immediate links: A=00100; B=00100; C=00010; D=00001; E=00000. An entry 1 means the row condition must precede the column condition. Reversing the convention is acceptable only if the whole matrix and interpretation are reversed consistently.

  2. Add implied reachability and the identity diagonal.

    Produce: A reachability matrix and one explained inferred link.

    Reachability including each condition itself is: A=10111; B=01111; C=00111; D=00011; E=00001. A reaches E through C and D, even without an immediate A-to-E link. A does not reach B and B does not reach A. Transitivity is appropriate to the stipulated prerequisite relation; it is not a licence to infer causation from arbitrary associations.

  3. Draw a layered model and identify the foundations.

    Produce: A dependency diagram with a stated direction.

    A and B form the initial foundation; both lead to C, then D, then E. With arrows drawn upward from prerequisites, E is the top-level dependent condition. Some diagrams reverse the visual layout: assess the meaning and arrows, not whether the page looks identical. Retain direct versus inferred links in the audit record.

  4. Return the model to the people who supplied the relation.

    Produce: Two questions that could change it.

    Ask whether the triage protocol can be designed before access permission but cannot be operated before it; that distinction could split C. Ask whether decision authority is needed for the whole allocation process or only disputed cases. A correct matrix can faithfully represent an oversimplified or disputed judgement. Record dissent and revise the elements or relation when necessary.

Checks

  1. Under the agreed prerequisite relation, does A reach E?

    • Yes, through C and D
    • No, because no direct A-to-E link was supplied
    • Only if A and B are merged

    Answer: Yes, through C and D. The reachability relation includes transitive paths. The immediate-link matrix and reachability matrix answer different questions.

  2. Does the matrix establish that A causes a successful referral outcome?

    • Yes
    • No: it represents agreed prerequisites among these conditions
    • Yes, because a binary model is objective

    Answer: No: it represents agreed prerequisites among these conditions. The relation and its source judgements bound the claim. Structural agreement is not empirical proof of outcome causation.

  3. Which pair has no reachability in either direction in this model?

    • A and B
    • C and E
    • D and E

    Answer: A and B. A and B are separate foundations. C reaches E through D, and D directly precedes E.

Repair

A matrix mixes 'must precede', 'is preferred to' and 'is funded by' without labelling the difference, then applies transitive closure.

Choose one explicit relation for a matrix, check whether transitive inference makes sense for it, and record the source judgements. Use separate representations for other relation types. Otherwise the calculation produces precise-looking nonsense.

Changed case

The group adds E before C: learning from review is needed to revise triage. What should you do rather than pretending the original acyclic sequence still holds?

C, D and E now form a cycle. Ask whether the model has mixed initial establishment with later operation. You might separate 'initial triage design' from 'triage revision', or represent an iterative subsystem explicitly. A cycle is a finding to interpret, not an error to delete for a prettier diagram.

Review criteria

  • The relation, direction and element meanings are explicit.
  • The immediate and reachability matrices are distinguished.
  • Inferred links and the independent A/B pair are correct.
  • The learner returns mathematical structure to participant judgement.

Interactive planning: design the service you would choose now

A small rehearsal of Ackoff's interactive planning and idealised design. It is not an invitation to ignore present feasibility, affected people or implementation.

Fictional case: residents must give the same household information separately to a repairs service, an advice service and a community transport service. Staff spend time chasing missing details. Residents avoid contact after repeated frustration. Each team meets its own response target by referring complicated cases elsewhere. The organisation is considering a faster referral form. For the design exercise, the existing arrangement has disappeared overnight, but current technology, legal responsibilities and today's environment remain. No unproven technology or compulsory data-sharing may be assumed.

  1. Formulate the mess rather than selecting one isolated symptom.

    Produce: Three mutually reinforcing problems and a consequence of continuing unchanged.

    Fragmented information creates repeated requests; repeated requests increase frustration and avoidance; avoidance creates incomplete information and more chasing. Local referral targets can move work without resolving need. A faster form may accelerate the same pattern. State this as a working account to investigate, not a proven history of a real organisation.

  2. Design an arrangement you would choose now if the old one no longer existed.

    Produce: A purposeful design with three capabilities and explicit constraints.

    One defensible design gives residents a supported entry route, a consent-based shared account of relevant needs, and a responsible person who coordinates agreed actions to completion. Use available technology and preserve lawful limits, refusal of sharing and alternatives to digital access. The design must remain capable of learning and changing; 'a perfect database that predicts every need' fails the exercise.

  3. Specify ends, means and resource implications.

    Produce: One desired result, two means and the resources or authority they require.

    End: residents obtain appropriate help without unnecessary repetition. Means could include a shared consent protocol and a case-coordination role. Resources include staff time, skills, a maintainable record, lawful access and decision authority. A new role title without time and rights is not a resource plan.

  4. Work back to a feasible first move and its control process.

    Produce: A small implementation step, prediction and review rule.

    With permission, trial coordinated handling of a defined set of cases using existing tools. Predict fewer repeated requests without inappropriate disclosure or delayed specialist support. Record resident effort, unresolved need and staff coordination time. Review weekly with participants, and revise or stop if harms emerge. Participation continues through design and adaptation rather than ending at consultation.

Checks

  1. Which proposal fits an idealised design under the stated constraints?

    • A service using currently available means that can learn and adapt
    • A perfect future AI requiring no consent
    • A forecast that present trends will continue

    Answer: A service using currently available means that can learn and adapt. The exercise designs a preferred feasible arrangement for the present environment. It is neither a technological fantasy nor an extrapolation of current trends.

  2. Why might a faster referral form leave the mess intact?

    • All forms are harmful
    • It may speed a process that still transfers rather than resolves need
    • Technology cannot improve services

    Answer: It may speed a process that still transfers rather than resolves need. The issue is the relationship among local targets, repeated information and unresolved needs. A form can help, but its effect depends on that arrangement.

Repair

The group writes a perfect future vision, assumes unlimited money and full consent, and labels implementation 'roll out'.

Bring present feasibility and environmental constraints back in. Specify resources, participation, implementation and ongoing learning. Preserve the ambitious redesign question while testing how action becomes possible.

Changed case

Residents reject a shared database but welcome a named coordinator. Does the design fail completely?

No. Separate the desired capability from one chosen means. A coordinator may support consented case-by-case information exchange without a universal database. Redesign the means with residents, and test the resulting workload and limits.

Review criteria

  • Interdependent problems are represented as a mess.
  • The design is desirable now, technologically feasible and adaptable.
  • Ends, means, resources and authority are distinguished.
  • Implementation includes participation and feedback, not just rollout.

Mosaic transformation and bubble strategy: sequence changes you can sustain

An original small rehearsal of published sequencing ideas. It does not reproduce a proprietary training exercise or claim that a protected bubble is a complete Mosaic transformation.

Fictional case: a service has two staff-days each week available for change, separate from its essential delivery work. A: remove duplicate reporting, needs two change-days in one week and releases two additional change-days per week from the following week. B: redesign triage, needs three change-days in one week and cannot start before A is complete. C: test a resident-led appointment process, needs four change-days in one week and cannot start before B. B and C release no additional capacity in this toy calculation. The released time is available only if managers stop demanding the old duplicate report. The C experiment needs a bounded group of cases and explicit interfaces with ordinary delivery.

  1. Build the smallest feasible sequence.

    Produce: A week-by-week capacity and dependency table.

    Week 1: available two days, spend two on A. Week 2: available four days if A's release is real, spend three on B. Week 3: available four days, spend four on C. No banking of unused days is assumed. Starting B or C in week 1 exceeds capacity, even if their expected benefits are larger.

  2. Check the assumption that makes the sequence possible.

    Produce: A release condition and an observable test.

    The old duplicate report must actually stop, and the freed time must be usable by the people needed for subsequent work. Check calendars, work records and staff experience after A. A nominal saving in one team's spreadsheet may not create available change capacity in another team.

  3. Define the C bubble without cutting it off from reality.

    Produce: Boundary, permissions, interfaces and stop/exit conditions.

    Specify eligible cases, resident consent, staff discretion, safety constraints and which usual rules may change. Agree hand-offs to specialists and ordinary delivery. Track whether work is displaced outside the bubble. Set conditions for stopping, adapting or incorporating what is learned. A permanent exception that depends on unpaid heroics is not a sustainable redesign.

  4. Compare a benefit-first ranking with a feasible change sequence.

    Produce: A short explanation of why the rankings differ.

    C may contribute most visibly to the end purpose, but cannot be done with present capacity or before B. A may be valuable partly because it creates the conditions for later work. Doability, contribution, capacity release and removed constraints all matter. The arithmetic does not settle political acceptability or prove any benefit will occur.

Checks

  1. When can B first start under the supplied assumptions?

    • Week 1
    • Week 2
    • Only after C

    Answer: Week 2. A must be complete and its released capacity available. That gives four change-days in week 2, enough for B's three.

  2. If managers continue the duplicate report, what is the week-2 capacity?

    • Four days regardless
    • Two days
    • Six days

    Answer: Two days. The assumed release has not occurred. The planned sequence is no longer feasible without another change or genuine extra capacity.

  3. What must a protected experiment retain?

    • No connection to ordinary delivery
    • Explicit interfaces and a route to learn, stop or integrate
    • A guarantee that its results will be scaled

    Answer: Explicit interfaces and a route to learn, stop or integrate. Protection creates room to test differences, not permission to conceal displaced costs or pre-commit to rollout.

Repair

The programme launches A, B and C together because each has a strong business case, using the same two staff-days three times.

Represent shared capacity once. Sequence prerequisites and verify released time before committing it. Make the next step conditional where the release or feasibility is uncertain.

Changed case

A releases only one usable change-day each week. Can the original sequence continue?

Week 2 has three days, enough for B. Week 3 also has three, insufficient for C's four-day indivisible task. C must be redesigned, postponed or given real additional capacity. Do not silently assume unused time or benefits can be borrowed from the future.

Review criteria

  • Capacity is counted once and release timing is explicit.
  • Dependencies and contribution are distinguished.
  • Released capacity is tested in actual work.
  • The bubble has boundaries, interfaces and exit conditions.

Multi-methodology: justify the move from one method to another

A basic methodological design exercise. There is no universal correct sequence. The checks concern fit, assumptions and transitions, not allegiance to one classification of methods.

Fictional case: a regional advice partnership faces rising waits. The sponsor calls this a capacity problem. Residents say they are referred repeatedly without help. Charities argue that eligibility rules exclude the people most in need. Service leads disagree about who can change the rules. Reliable arrival and completion counts exist for two services, but not for the whole partnership. The sponsor wants a recommendation in four weeks.

  1. Separate the questions that require different kinds of inquiry.

    Produce: Four questions, each with a needed output.

    Whose purpose and experience count? A boundary and perspective account. What purposeful activity is relevant? Alternative activity models and comparison questions. How do queues behave under stated rules? A bounded stock-flow model with tested data. Who can coordinate, allocate resources and adapt? A governance-function diagnosis. A single diagram need not answer all four.

  2. Choose a justified three-method sequence.

    Produce: Method, question, output and limitation for each stage.

    One defensible sequence is CSH to expose beneficiary and evidence boundaries; SSM to explore competing purposeful activities; then SD to test a particular queue mechanism where data support it. Another defensible design might use CSH, VSM and SD if governance failure is central. The order depends on what is learned. State what your choice omits and why that is tolerable for this inquiry.

  3. Specify the transition into the numerical model.

    Produce: One translation rule and one thing that must not be translated automatically.

    Use an agreed operational definition of a case, entry, completion and time period before constructing flows. An SSM activity named 'support a resident to obtain help' is not automatically a measured outflow. Normative CSH claims are not parameter estimates. Keep excluded people and disputed purposes visible beside the quantitative boundary.

  4. Set a trigger to change your method design.

    Produce: A revision trigger and a stopping rule.

    If case tracing shows that apparent completions are referrals that return elsewhere, revise the SD boundary and data plan. If no legitimate decision-maker can act on a proposed change, investigate governance and authority rather than producing more forecasts. Stop adding methods when they no longer change the decision or inquiry; document residual uncertainty.

Checks

  1. Which is a sound reason to add a method?

    • It makes the report look comprehensive
    • It answers an important question the current approach cannot adequately address
    • It is the facilitator's favourite

    Answer: It answers an important question the current approach cannot adequately address. Method selection needs a purpose and a limit. More methods do not by themselves create a better inquiry.

  2. Can a purposeful activity from SSM be treated immediately as a calibrated SD variable?

    • Yes, both are systems methods
    • No, its meaning, measurement and modelling role must be established
    • Yes, provided the label is unchanged

    Answer: No, its meaning, measurement and modelling role must be established. The representations perform different jobs. Translation requires operational definitions and evidence, not shared vocabulary alone.

  3. Two learners choose different method sequences. Must one be wrong?

    • Yes
    • No, compare their purposes, assumptions, omissions and revision rules
    • No, every sequence is equally useful

    Answer: No, compare their purposes, assumptions, omissions and revision rules. Multiple designs can be defensible, but they are not beyond criticism. Inspect what each makes possible and what it misses.

Repair

'We will do SSM, VSM, SD and CSH, then combine the diagrams into one master model.'

State the distinct question each approach serves, how its result informs the next, what assumptions change in translation, and how disagreement will survive. A pile of methods is not an integrated inquiry.

Changed case

The sponsor withdraws access to residents but still asks for a whole-system recommendation. What changes?

The participation and evidence boundary has materially narrowed. State the limitation, challenge it constructively and seek a workable alternative such as supported independent input. A staff-only exercise can continue with a narrower claim; it cannot honestly become the residents' validated account.

Review criteria

  • Every selected method has a distinct inquiry purpose.
  • Limitations and omitted questions are explicit.
  • Transitions preserve differences between normative, conceptual and empirical claims.
  • New evidence can change the sequence and scope.

Patterns of Strategy: model dependence, fit and timing

An original introductory relationship exercise. It does not reproduce the book's pattern catalogue or claim that one numerical ratio captures strategic power.

Fictional case: a local repair network needs a specialist supplier to complete 60% of its jobs. The network accounts for only 5% of that supplier's sales. Finding and qualifying an alternative supplier would take six months. The network has promised residents completion within one month. The supplier can change its delivery terms with two months' notice. Neighbouring networks use the same supplier but have not agreed to cooperate. A proposal says 'negotiate harder because we are a large organisation'.

  1. Map the strategic relationship from both sides.

    Produce: A two-direction dependence account with the purpose of each actor.

    The repair network depends heavily on this supplier for its present offering. The supplier has relatively little sales dependence on this one network. The percentages are indicators of asymmetry, not a complete power measure: margins, scarce expertise, switching costs and reputation could change the assessment. The network's organisational size alone does not establish bargaining strength.

  2. Test temporal fit.

    Produce: A comparison of notice, response time and the resident promise.

    Two months' notice is shorter than the six months needed to qualify an alternative, leaving a four-month response gap under the stated assumptions. The one-month completion promise may be incompatible with supply disruption. A strategy that requires switching instantly has failed a timing test before any negotiation begins.

  3. Generate two moves that change the relationship rather than merely state an ambition.

    Produce: Two moves, their preconditions and likely counterpart responses.

    Begin qualifying an alternative before disruption, accepting the cost of maintaining options. Or explore joint purchasing and shared qualification with neighbouring networks, but only if they agree and the arrangement is lawful and practical. The supplier may respond through service guarantees, pricing or prioritisation. Do not treat neighbours' resources as already controlled by the network.

  4. Specify the evidence that would change your strategic choice.

    Produce: A signal and a conditional decision.

    If a second supplier can meet quality requirements within three months rather than six, the response gap changes. If neighbours cannot cooperate, discard the assumed collective bargaining position. Track supplier concentration, qualification progress and actual resident waiting rather than reporting the number of negotiation meetings.

Checks

  1. What is the stated gap between notice and alternative qualification?

    • Four months
    • One month
    • Eight months

    Answer: Four months. Six months to qualify minus two months' notice leaves four months without a ready alternative, assuming qualification starts at notice.

  2. Do neighbouring networks' purchases automatically increase this network's power?

    • Yes
    • No: cooperation and decision rights are not established
    • Only if added to a spreadsheet

    Answer: No: cooperation and decision rights are not established. Potential collective action is not an existing resource or commitment.

  3. Which is a strategic move rather than an aspiration?

    • Become the best repair network
    • Qualify a second supplier before the current contract becomes vulnerable
    • Demand excellence

    Answer: Qualify a second supplier before the current contract becomes vulnerable. The move changes a dependency and has a timing, cost and feasibility test. The other statements do not specify a change in the relationship.

Repair

A SWOT list declares 'strength: we are big' and concludes the supplier must accept any terms.

Examine the particular relationship, each side's alternatives, dependence, fit and response time. Organisational size is not identical to power in every relationship.

Changed case

The supplier now depends on this network for 40% of its sales. What follows, and what does not?

The relationship may be more mutually dependent, creating different negotiation possibilities and risks. It does not prove the network should threaten withdrawal or that the supplier has no alternatives. Investigate profitability, capacity, other customers and the cost of damaging the relationship.

Review criteria

  • The relationship is represented from both actors' positions.
  • Dependence and timing are tested with the supplied facts.
  • Moves alter relationships or options rather than restating goals.
  • Uncommitted partners and uncertain responses are not treated as controlled resources.

Socio-Technical Systems: redesign the work and technology together

A basic primary-work-system exercise informed by the socio-technical tradition. It is not a complete participative design process or a substitute for workers' and users' involvement.

Fictional case: a repair service introduces handheld job software. The software permits only one fault code per visit. Workers often find several linked faults, but cannot alter the code or order additional parts. A supervisor can approve changes the next morning. Residents then receive extra appointments. Management proposes training workers to comply more consistently. Workers propose discretion to record linked faults, while the safety lead insists that electrical work remains restricted to qualified staff.

  1. Define the primary task and the social and technical arrangements.

    Produce: A short work-system account rather than a technology inventory.

    Primary task: complete agreed safe repairs in residents' homes. Technical arrangements include fault coding, devices, records and parts ordering. Social arrangements include skills, authority, division of work, supervision and resident communication. These interact: a software field can constrain discretion, and an authority rule can turn a technically possible update into a day's delay.

  2. Locate a significant variance and where it is currently controlled.

    Produce: Four bipolar or contrast-explicit concepts, retaining the speaker's meaning.

    Produce: A directed map plus a statement explaining every arrow.

    One proposed chain is bounded discretion -> fewer approval waits -> fewer repeat calls -> more time for resolution. Another is appropriate spending control -> budget viability. These links represent supplied or inferred reasoning, with inference labelled.

    Produce: One option and a question for negotiation.

    • Some approvals may contribute to repeat demand while others protect a valued constraint
    • Finance is opposed to residents receiving help

  2. What is the status of an arrow derived from an interview statement?

    • A proven physical law
    • A vote

The facilitator merges 'financial control' and 'bureaucracy' into one negative concept and reports consensus.

  • Arrows form explainable means-ends reasoning.

    Produce: Three judgements about what counts as a good result, without forcing one metric.

    Prompt closure, sustained relationship and a usable answer are different appreciations of the situation. Ask what experiences and standards sustain each judgement. This is an appreciative-systems inquiry in Vickers' sense; it is not simply a request to think positively.

    Produce: Evidence of enabling conditions and a question about transfer.

    Ask what the named decision-maker could actually do, what trust and access made possible, and whether the arrangement depended on unusual unpaid effort.     Produce: A jointly owned question, small action, observation and reflection.

    • Only during Appreciative Inquiry

    Probe 1 offers a choice of appointment times to a consenting small group, testing timing constraints. A basic mixed-evidence evaluation rehearsal. It is not a complete impact evaluation, statistical inference course or assessment of real practitioner behaviour.

Fictional case: after a new triage process, recorded average waiting time falls from 20 to 12 days. During the same period, 30 difficult cases are moved to another team and excluded from the report. Staff report less duplicated work. Three residents describe improved clarity; two describe feeling dismissed. The analyst designed the change and chose the waiting-time measure.     Produce: An absolute change, a percentage change and a qualification.

  2. Build a small evaluation matrix.

    Produce: Outcome, process, distribution and unintended-effect evidence.

    The analyst selected both intervention and measure and may favour evidence of improvement. Use an independent challenge or joint review, retain negative cases, document changed definitions and publish the measurement boundary. Reflexivity is not a confession that makes evidence unnecessary.

  4. Decide what can responsibly be reported now.

    Produce: A short finding and a next learning action.

    'Recorded waiting fell, and some staff and residents report benefits. Changed case boundaries and mixed resident experience prevent a whole-service causal claim. We will trace the transferred cases and review dismissal accounts before expanding.' This supports action without manufacturing certainty.

Checks

  1. What is the reported percentage reduction from 20 to 12?

    • 8%
    • 40%
    • 60%

    Answer: 40%. The reduction is (20-12)/20 = 0.4. The new value is 60% of the old value; that is not the reduction.

  2. Which finding is currently defensible?

    • Triage caused a 40% improvement for everyone
    • The recorded average fell, but the causal and population claim remains uncertain
    • Nothing can be learned

    Answer: The recorded average fell, but the causal and population claim remains uncertain. There is useful evidence, but its boundary and competing explanations limit the conclusion.

  3. What should happen to the two negative resident accounts?

    • Remove them as outliers
    • Investigate and retain them as possible evidence of unintended effects
    • Treat them as proof all results are false

    Answer: Investigate and retain them as possible evidence of unintended effects. Neither erasure nor automatic generalisation is warranted. The accounts can change the evaluation questions and the intervention.

Repair

The evaluator calls the pilot successful because the selected indicator improved, while excluding transferred cases and dissenting accounts.

Restore the end-to-end boundary, explain changes in inclusion and consider alternative explanations. Evaluate effects and experience, not only the measure chosen by the designer.

Changed case

Transferred cases now show waits of 50 days. What does that tell you, and what else is needed?

It reveals a potentially serious displaced burden. To calculate a combined mean, obtain counts and comparable definitions for both groups; do not average the two means without weights. Examine whether transfer was appropriate and what happened to residents.

Review criteria

  • The arithmetic is correct.
  • Description, causation and generalisation are distinguished.
  • Displaced and negative effects remain in view.
  • The evaluator's role changes the safeguards and inquiry.

Systemic constellations: use a spatial representation as a hypothesis

A low-stakes tabletop orientation exercise only, not a full constellation facilitation or therapy. Spatial impressions are not evidence of absent people's feelings, motives or hidden historical facts. Use trained support for deeper work and never compel personal disclosure.

Fictional case: a project coordinator receives requests from a sponsor and three service teams. The sponsor's messages are frequent; the teams' concerns often arrive late. Residents are discussed but do not attend project meetings. Use five labelled objects: coordinator, sponsor, teams, residents and project purpose. You may instead draw the arrangement or describe it in text; no movement or personal disclosure is required.

  1. Arrange the objects to represent one interpretation of attention and contact.

    Produce: A diagram or text account with a stated meaning for distance and direction.

    One possible arrangement puts the sponsor close to the coordinator, teams farther away and residents outside the main line of attention. Label this as your construction from the case, not a discovered invisible order. Another arrangement can be defensible if its meaning is explicit.

  2. Describe before interpreting.

    Produce: Two observations about the arrangement and two hypotheses about work.

    Observation: the resident object is outside the line between sponsor and coordinator. Hypothesis: resident experience may receive less attention. Observation: the sponsor is placed closest. Hypothesis: frequent sponsor requests may dominate the coordinator's time. Neither placement establishes what the real people feel or intend.

  3. Try a different arrangement and identify a real-world test.

    Produce: One changed relationship and one observable inquiry.

    Move purpose and residents into view and ask what would make that meaningful in work. A test could be to bring a consented resident account into a project review and observe whether it changes decisions. Moving an object is not itself an organisational change or proof of a better relationship.

Checks

  1. What can the tabletop arrangement directly establish?

    • The sponsor's hidden motives
    • The modeller's current representation and questions it raises
    • The feelings of residents who are absent

    Answer: The modeller's current representation and questions it raises. It externalises an interpretation. Claims about people or events require appropriate evidence and consent.

  2. May a learner decline embodied or personal participation?

    • Yes, use drawing or text and respect the boundary
    • No, refusal proves resistance
    • Only if the facilitator approves the reason

    Answer: Yes, use drawing or text and respect the boundary. This rehearsal does not require personal exposure or physical participation. Refusal must not be turned into a diagnosis.

Repair

The facilitator says 'the objects prove the sponsor resents residents' and instructs the coordinator to confront them.

Withdraw the unsupported claim. Separate placement, impression and inference. Turn the impression into a cautious question that can be checked through ordinary inquiry; do not accuse an absent person on this basis.

Changed case

A second learner places the teams closest to the coordinator. How should you compare the models?

Ask what each distance means and which case observations informed it. They may represent frequency, emotional salience or authority differently. Compare meanings and evidence rather than declaring one placement the true system.

Review criteria

  • The representation's conventions and author are explicit.
  • Impressions are not treated as facts about absent people.
  • Participation is optional and bounded.
  • The exercise generates a testable inquiry rather than a diagnosis.

Systemic conversations: expose inference and repair a broken commitment

An original conversation exercise bridging several distinct practices. It does not certify coaching or facilitation skill, and should not be used to compel disclosure of personal or confidential material.

Fictional transcript: Sam says 'Could you get the referral picture sorted soon?' Lee replies 'I'll see what I can do.' Three days later Sam says 'You never follow through.' Lee says 'You keep changing what you want.' Sam privately thinks Lee is avoiding responsibility. Lee privately thinks Sam wants a complete service redesign, which Lee cannot authorise. No deadline, output, quality criterion or decision right was agreed.

  1. Separate what was said from what was inferred.

    Produce: A two-column observation/inference record.

    The quoted statements are supplied observations. Avoidance and an expectation of complete redesign are inferences. 'Never' and 'keep changing' are generalisations not established by this short transcript. Ask for examples without denying the speakers' experience.

  2. Map the commitment sequence.

    Produce: Request, clarification, promise, completion and satisfaction, marking absent stages.

    There is an ambiguous request and a non-committal response. Clarification and a firm promise are absent. No agreed completion test exists, so later dissatisfaction cannot fairly be treated as breach of a clearly specified promise. This does not establish that either person has no responsibility; it locates the missing conversation.

  3. Draft a repair that combines advocacy and inquiry.

    Produce: A short replacement exchange.

    Sam: 'I expected a one-page map by Friday, but I did not specify that. What did you understand, and what can you commit to?' Lee: 'I understood a redesign. I can map the current referral routes by Friday if the three leads provide examples; I cannot approve changes. Shall we agree that scope?' The repair exposes reasoning and negotiates a feasible commitment.

  4. Name an undiscussable safely.

    Produce: One observation and an invitation, without a forced confession.

    For example: 'We seem to agree quickly and discover different expectations later. Could we examine one instance and how our authority and workload affect what we promise?' Do not announce that you have diagnosed everyone's hidden motives.

Checks

  1. Is 'I'll see what I can do' an unambiguous promise of a complete redesign by Friday?

    • Yes
    • No
    • Yes, because Sam is senior

    Answer: No. The transcript contains no such scope or date. Seniority does not fill in unspoken terms.

  2. Which is an inference rather than a supplied observation?

    • Lee said 'I'll see what I can do'
    • Lee is avoiding responsibility
    • Sam asked for the referral picture to be sorted

    Answer: Lee is avoiding responsibility. Avoidance is a proposed explanation. It may be investigated, but is not established by the words alone.

Repair

The coach instructs Lee to admit avoidance and labels any disagreement a defensive routine.

Make the coach's interpretation contestable. Return to examples, test alternative explanations and negotiate clearer commitments. A method that makes disagreement proof of its own diagnosis prevents learning.

Changed case

Lee now promises the one-page map, but a service lead refuses access. What should happen before Friday?

Lee should communicate the changed condition, explain the effect and renegotiate scope, support or timing. Keeping silent until the deadline sustains the same pattern. Sam should address authority and access rather than treating every dependency as Lee's personal failure.

Review criteria

  • Observation and inference are distinguished.
  • Missing commitment stages are identified.
  • The repair includes scope, timing, conditions and authority.
  • The helper's own interpretation remains challengeable.
