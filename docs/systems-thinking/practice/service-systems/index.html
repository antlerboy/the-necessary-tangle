Systemic intervention | about 40 minutes | free

Service systems: follow demand and distinguish activity from resolution

Classify a small demand sample, trace end-to-end work, and design a prediction-led test.

Framework link: Deming; Vanguard Method (Seddon); Lean & Six-Sigma. Apprenticeship practice links: K1, K3, K5, S1, S3, S6, S7, S11.

What this exercise can and cannot establish

A bridge to systemic service improvement, not a full Vanguard, Lean or Six Sigma course. These traditions are distinct; this exercise does not validate statistical process-control competence.

This is original learning material, not a reproduced official course. Mechanical checks have determinate answers within the stated case. Worked comparisons for open judgements show defensible reasoning, not the only possible model. Specialist pedagogical review is not recorded.

The case

Fictional case: ten consecutive calls to a repair desk are recorded. Calls 1, 2, 4 and 8 request new repairs. Calls 3, 5 and 9 ask why an agreed visit did not happen. Calls 6 and 10 report that a supposedly completed repair still leaks. Call 7 asks for an unrelated general-information leaflet. Staff are measured on average handling time. One repair trace shows three minutes on the first call, two days waiting for approval, a failed visit because the wrong part was sent, and two more calls before a completed repair eight days later.

Work through it

Use paper, your own drawing tool or the notes below. Attempt each step before opening its comparison. Describe a diagram in words when that is more accessible.

  1. 1. Classify the demand using the reason for contact.

    Produce: A ten-row classification with a stated definition.

    Compare your work for step 1

    Four calls request new repairs. Five arise from something the service failed to do or do right: three missed visits and two ineffective repairs. One is a separate information request whose relevance depends on the service purpose. The five repeated-failure contacts are 50% of this tiny sample, not a proven organisation-wide rate. Never infer demand type from the caller's attitude.

  2. 2. Trace value, waiting and rework end to end.

    Produce: A resident-centred work trace.

    Compare your work for step 2

    Follow need to completed repair, including approval delay, wrong parts, failed visit and repeat contact. Three minutes of call handling does not represent eight days of elapsed experience. Reducing the first call to two minutes could worsen diagnosis and increase total work.

  3. 3. Design a PDSA test rather than a rollout.

    Produce: Prediction, bounded action, evidence and decision.

    Compare your work for step 3

    Predict that confirming the fault and required part before dispatch reduces failed visits in a small agreed set of jobs without creating excessive delay. Test, compare actual results with the prediction, and revise the explanation or practice. Include resident resolution and staff effort. A before-after improvement alone does not isolate the cause.

Check the basic distinctions

These checks test the supplied case, not your overall competence. Open answers and model variants still need your judgement.

1. How many calls in the sample arose from missed visits or ineffective repairs?

Answer and reasoning 1

Five

Calls 3, 5, 9, 6 and 10 are the five specified failure-related contacts.

2. Does the sample prove 50% of all future demand is failure-related?

Answer and reasoning 2

No, sample size, timing and representativeness matter

The sample supports a local observation and further inquiry, not an exact population estimate.

3. What belongs in the Study step of PDSA?

Answer and reasoning 3

Compare observations with the prediction and revise the theory

Prediction-led learning is different from checking implementation alone.

Repair a defective model or claim

'Calls are waste. Block repeat callers and the demand problem will disappear.'
Compare your repair

Investigate why people call. Prevent avoidable failures and resolve legitimate need. Suppressing contact can hide unresolved work and increase harm elsewhere.

Try a changed case before looking

Handling time rises by one minute while failed visits fall. Can the change still be worthwhile?

Compare the changed case

Yes, potentially. Compare safe resolution, total end-to-end effort, repeat contact and resident experience. The local time measure alone cannot decide. Quantify the actual effects before concluding.

Review the process, not just the score

  • Demand is classified from purpose and cause, not blame.
  • A sample is not treated as a population certainty.
  • End-to-end resolution remains visible.
  • The test includes a prediction and a learning decision.

A different model can be defensible when its purpose, assumptions and reasoning are explicit. A contradiction, incorrect unit, invented fact or unacknowledged change of purpose needs repair. Keep both your first attempt and revision.
