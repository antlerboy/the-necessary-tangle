Systems leadership: make inquiry actionable

Systems leadership can include making inquiry and action possible when the patterns producing a problem cross organisational boundaries. This route develops Benjamin P Taylor’s published argument into questions for practice. It is an attributed position, not a consensus definition of the field.

Start with something that keeps happening

Describe the recurring experience in words that the people affected would recognise. Trace the decisions, rules, resource arrangements, interactions, and delays that help produce it. Test that explanation against another account. A map which many people recognise can still omit the people carrying its costs.

Compare Feedback, Boundary, and Modelling.

Follow the pattern to the authority

Identify a concrete condition that could change: a referral rule, an approval threshold, a contract, a resource allocation, or the way competing obligations are settled. Find who can change it, what stops them, and whose cooperation they need. The person closest to the difficulty may know most about it while controlling very little of what maintains it.

Bring that person’s evidence to a decision in a form that shows the consequences and the available choices. Where nobody has sole authority, the practical work includes convening the parties who control different conditions and agreeing what each will change. An invitation to collaborate does not itself confer authority or settle a conflict of purpose.

Distinguish an exception from a changed arrangement

A senior intervention may help one person receive support. Follow the next comparable case. If it encounters the same barrier, the original intervention left the recurring mechanism largely intact. Useful learning includes finding the rule or relationship which allowed the exception, and deciding whether it should change for others.

Be precise about what was improved: one outcome, the handling of comparable cases, or the conditions producing those cases. These are different claims and need different evidence.

Keep inquiry inside the action

Agree what will change, who will act, when the effects should become visible, and what would justify revising the explanation. Check whether delay or risk moved somewhere else. Give affected people a route to challenge both the account and the change. Our own framing, commitments, and participation belong in that examination.
