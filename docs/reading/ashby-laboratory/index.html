Ashby Laboratory: states, stability and learning from a black box

Open the Ashby Laboratory to explore interactive adaptations of chapters 3–6 of W. Ross Ashby’s An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956). The application contains 56 exercises and 45 glossary entries. These four pathways help you choose a question and connect it to the atlas.

An independent interactive learning resource adapting concepts from W. Ross Ashby's An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956). Authorship of the original book belongs to Ashby; the modern exercises are adaptations.

Open the root application, choose the chapter, then the named exercise. The application does not implement stable exercise URLs.

Chapter 3 · 12 exercises

Describe states and follow transformations

Explore whether a state description is sufficient to predict the next state. Compare transformations, closure, trajectories, basins, equilibria and cycles; distinguish a phase plot from a time plot, determinism from reversibility, and convergence from finite arrival. Vector updates happen simultaneously.

Try this question: Would two systems with the same recorded state always have the same next recorded state?

Chapter 4 · 17 exercises

Investigate inputs, coupling and boundaries

Inputs select transformations, and different controls may produce the same result. Test clamping, coupling, rewiring, transients, delays and reachability. Compare a diagram of component dependencies with a graph of complete states. What counts as input or output depends on the chosen boundary.

Chapter 5 · 13 exercises

Test what stability means

A set can remain stable while its members continue to move. Explore closure under composition, stability relative to specified disturbances, neutral equilibrium and bus bunching. The sign of a feedback loop alone does not establish the stability of the whole system.

Try this question: Stable with respect to which disturbance, and over which set of states?

Chapter 6 · 14 exercises

Learn from a black box without inventing its behaviour

Build observation protocols and choose experiments that distinguish competing accounts. Keep unknown transitions unknown and distinguish inaccessible states from untested ones. Compare isomorphism with coarse-graining, test which distinctions a weak model loses, and use observation history when the current recorded state is insufficient.

Try this question: Which next observation would distinguish the models, and what has your simplification hidden?
