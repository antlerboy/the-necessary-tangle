Schot: socio-technical transitions and the conditions of change

Johan Schot’s work asks how ways of meeting social needs change, and why similar directions of change recur across different socio-technical systems. Technology, institutions, markets, practices and infrastructure are considered together.

Three distinctions to keep

The multi-level perspective examines interactions between innovation niches, established regimes and wider landscape pressures. These are analytical levels, not management ranks. A regime includes established rules and practices; it is more than the organisations currently delivering a service.

Deep Transitions extends the question across systems and longer historical periods. A rule can organise one system; a meta-rule is shared across systems; a meta-regime is a shared rule-set. The proposed sustainable Second Deep Transition is an ambition and research proposition, not an assured historical outcome.

A successful experiment and a changed regime are different achievements. Asking which rules select, fund and retain an innovation can sharpen inquiry, but does not remove questions of power, legitimacy or contested purpose.

Editorial application: why does a pilot stay exceptional?

This public-service exercise is an original editorial application, not a case studied by Schot or a claim that his framework is proven in this setting.

A fictional relational service helps residents, but depends on temporary funding and staff bypassing ordinary rules. Compare two explanations: the surrounding rules prevent the practice becoming ordinary; or the practice works only under unusually favourable conditions.

  1. Name one rule: a budget condition, eligibility criterion, measure, professional boundary or decision right. How is it enforced? Who can change it?
  2. Trace whether the same rule also shapes another service. A resemblance is a lead to test, not enough to establish a shared meta-rule.
  3. Ask who defines success, whose experience is missing and who may challenge the purpose. Improvement inside a team can shift burdens onto residents.
  4. Change one condition and observe whether the benefit survives without exceptional workarounds. Specify a result that would challenge your explanation.
Compare a possible response

Track the resident’s benefit alongside the effort and exceptions needed to produce it. Test a revised funding or decision rule across two settings before treating it as a general explanation. Retain disagreement about the desired outcome. A historical theory can guide questions; it cannot grant authority to impose the answers.
