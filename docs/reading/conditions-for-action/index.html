What makes action possible?

Part of the SysPrac26 collection, including Benjamin P Taylor’s two follow-up workshops.

Six sessions from the SCiO Systems Practice 2026 programme offer different ways to investigate conditions for action. This comparison is editorial: the speakers do not present a common framework, and the sequence does not imply influence. The accounts await human editorial acceptance.

What makes action possible?

Kai-Uwe Radde treats deferred organisational work as six kinds of debt. His influence map is subjective and should be rebuilt for the situation; his team questions ask what is missing, what obstructs progress, and what would help, followed by a later check. The lens does not establish accounting recognition, completeness, or a measured improvement. Radde, 29:12–31:04; presentation, slides 14–20 and 29–36.

Max Shein asks what must move with responsibility: capability, authority, relationships, and institutional memory. In his situated NHS transition hypotheses, he describes revising a diagnosis from failure to notice a problem towards a question of sustained priority and resources. The proposed assurance cycle is illustrative, not an evaluated causal finding or a statement of current NHS conditions. Shein, 11:10–11:58 and 13:07–15:19.

Autonomy means different things

Ivo Velitchkov distinguishes discretion within a coordination arrangement from the opportunities that participation enables. He proposes that control over coordination terms tends to concentrate when participants cannot readily replace the arrangement; counter-action may create another concentration. This is an attributed, contestable proposal. The thermodynamics comparison is a mnemonic, and the article does not establish concentration alone as a cause of collapse. Velitchkov, 12:31–17:19 and 20:45–24:21; article; Cohesion Spectrum v.2; The Second Law of Technodynamics — Velitchkov (2026).

Jan De Visch uses autonomy and cohesion in a human-developmental account, and asks which relationships sustaining value creation are becoming fragile. His meaning is not interchangeable with Velitchkov's socio-technical distinction. The cases illustrate his proposal; they do not demonstrate regenerative outcomes. De Visch, 11:55–15:24 and 21:53–22:54; presentation, pages 2–7.

What is shared when words are shared?

Steve Whitla distinguishes the substantive meaning of a representation from what using or challenging it communicates about identity and belonging. His proposed responses include curious questioning, reconnection with experience, and practice in Clean Language. His discussion of POSIWID examines attachment to a phrase; it does not settle the meaning of purpose. Whitla, 09:38–12:40, 14:15–19:00, and 20:00–24:18.

Which conditions could defeat a strategy?

Mark Workman presents a way to explore conditions under which a strategy might fail stakeholder goals. His portfolio example exposes dependencies and invites people to challenge assumptions. Its numerical and performance assertions are unverified, and the models were not available for this review. Workman, 11:49–14:01 and 20:09–25:49.

For a live decision, the reader might ask: what work has been deferred; who has the capability and authority to act; who can change participation terms; what do people understand by the words they share; and what conditions would defeat the strategy? These questions combine an editorial selection, not a jointly authored method.

Source limits

The public session descriptions and recovered auto-generated captions were read. The caption passages have not been checked against audio or on-screen visuals. The member presentations by Shein, Whitla, and Workman were unavailable; Workman’s models were unavailable. The older Productive Organisational Paradoxes deck linked with Velitchkov’s session is a different presentation. Broader theoretical attributions and unverified numerical claims are deferred.
