What crosses a boundary? Regulation, learning, and transduction

A referral becomes a category. A worker's judgement becomes a number. A number reaches a meeting, where someone may be able to act on it. Follow that passage and several questions appear: what distinctions survive, who can respond, what they can change, and how the organisations themselves are changed by the relationship.

These three reading routes connect those questions to primary texts. Comparisons are identified as comparisons. Their order does not establish a lineage between authors.

What must a regulator distinguish?

Ashby's law concerns a specified relation between disturbances, responses, and outcomes. It asks how much variety can remain in the outcomes when a regulator can select among different responses. The transformation being regulated matters too: section 11/9 allows for a transformation that already removes some variety. Counting everything in an environment and demanding an equally complicated organisation misses this qualification.

Read An Introduction to Cybernetics, sections 11/5–11/11, especially 11/9. The electronic copy places two printed pages on each PDF page. Continue with Requisite variety and Law of Requisite Variety.

A model that regulates

Conant and Ashby ask a different question: what form does a simplest optimal regulator take? Their result concerns a mapping from events in the system to regulatory actions, under a specified probability model and an outcome-entropy criterion. A model can be embodied in how a regulator acts; it need not be a conscious simulation. Possessing a diagram establishes neither optimality nor effective regulation.

Read Every Good Regulator of a System Must Be a Model of That System and the open electronic transcription, theorem and proof on PDF pp. 8–9, followed by the discussion. Some equations in the transcription have formatting faults. Inspect Conant-Ashby Theorem and Modelling.

Capacity at the crossing

Beer distinguishes variety in operations and management from the capacity of their channels and boundary transducers. An adequate response repertoire can be defeated by an inadequate route to it. His third Principle of Organization places a variety requirement on the transducer whenever a channel crosses a boundary. The practical question is which distinctions the conversion can preserve and what the receiver can do with them.

Read The Viable System Model: its provenance, development, methodology and pathology, PDF pp. 6–8 and Appendix 2, p. 16. This is a 2002 electronic edition of a text dated 1989, not the separately published 1984 article. Continue with Transduction, Viable System Model (VSM), and Organisational recursion.

Practice comparison: follow one troublesome case across a service boundary. Record what was noticed, what the receiving form could represent, who could authorise a response, and what happened. A signal may arrive perfectly and still reach someone unable to act. These are questions for inquiry, not findings about a particular service.

What changes when we learn?

Bateson places information within a circuit in which selected differences have effects. Sending the same report to more people does not establish what any recipient learned. Shannon's mathematical treatment addresses a different problem: uncertainty and communication. It does not supply a quantity of understanding or settle what a message means.

Read Steps to an Ecology of Mind (1987 reprint), 'Form, Substance, and Difference', printed pp. 459–460, alongside A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Introduction, corrected reprint PDF p. 1. Compare Information and Information theory. The atlas connection states the scope of mathematical information without treating it as a general formalisation of meaning.

Changes within learning, and changes to learning

Bateson's Learning I involves correction within a set of alternatives. Learning II changes the process: for example, the alternatives or the way a sequence of experience is punctuated. A learned premise can become persistent and self-confirming. Calling a change 'higher-order' therefore does not establish that it is beneficial.

Read 'The Logical Categories of Learning and Communication', printed pp. 297–299 and 305–307 in the same 1987 reprint. These page numbers should not be transferred to another edition unchecked. Continue with Bateson's levels of learning.

Which governing assumptions can change?

Argyris examines organisational inquiry through single-loop and double-loop models. The former can correct action while retaining a governing framework; the latter can put that framework in question. His account also distinguishes what people say guides them from the theories-in-use evident in their conduct. Adopting the vocabulary of inquiry does not by itself change how a challenge is treated.

Read Single-Loop and Double-Loop Models in Research on Decision Making, p. 367 and pp. 368–370. The publisher record verifies the article; the inspected page-marked reading copy is a third-party transcription, identified in the source record below. Continue with Single-loop learning and Double-loop learning.

Editorial comparison: Bateson and Argyris both help distinguish changing a response from changing premises through which responses are selected. Their classifications have different purposes and scope. Learning II is not simply a replacement name for double-loop learning, and this comparison makes no claim about direct influence.

In practice, ask for an example. What would the organisation previously have done, what did it do this time, and which assumption was made open to change? The evidence might show improved action within a framework, a changed framework, or only a new account of unchanged conduct.

Does the boundary separate things already formed?

In Beer's organisational account, transduction concerns a conversion across a boundary. Simondon uses the same word to address individuation: how an individual takes form. His simplest example is crystallisation, where a region already structured enables further structuring. The relation is involved in the formation of its terms. Treating this as another name for message translation would lose the question his account asks.

Read The Position of the Problem of Ontogenesis, printed p. 11 (PDF page 8), then p. 12. This is Gregory Flanders's 2009 translation; the date does not identify the origin of Simondon's theory. Continue with Transduction in Simondon and Gilbert Simondon.

A history of interaction

Maturana's structural coupling concerns recurrent interaction and structural change while the organisation defining each unity is conserved. A perturbation can trigger change without specifying the response: the system's current structure matters. Applying this question to communication asks us to examine the recipient and the history of the relationship as well as the message. That organisational comparison needs evidence of its own.

Read The Organization of the Living: A Theory of the Living Organization, 'Plasticity and structural coupling', pp. 155–156, and 'Structural coupling', pp. 162–163, in the inspected 1999 reprint. Continue with Structural coupling. The original 1975 article has different pagination.

A boundary produced by the unity

Varela, Maturana, and Uribe's account of autopoiesis specifies a network of production whose components regenerate the network and constitute a distinguishable unity. In the biological account, boundary production participates in that organisation. This is more specific than a system surviving, reproducing, or organising itself. Dependence on a medium and exchanges of material remain compatible with it.

Read Autopoiesis: The Organization of Living Systems, Its Characterization and a Model, section 3, p. 188; section 7, pp. 191–192; and the identification key in section 9, pp. 192–193. Maturana's reprint, p. 155, states the dependence on a medium. Continue with Autopoiesis, Autopoiesis: The Organization of Living Systems, Its Characterization, and a Model, and Boundary.

Editorial comparison: a boundary may be drawn by an observer, maintained through work, or produced by the organisation being described. These possibilities ask different questions of evidence. Before calling a service or institution autopoietic, specify its components, their production, and what constitutes its unity. A biological metaphor cannot do that work on its own.

A joint form may translate information between two services. Repeated work around it may also change what each service notices, permits, and becomes. That is a reason to investigate both the conversion and the relationship, without assuming that all four theoretical accounts explain the same mechanism.
