Regulation

A relation among disturbances, a reguland, a regulator and acceptable outcomes in which outcome variety is constrained relative to a stated goal or essential-variable range.

Summary

Regulation is the work of keeping outcomes within acceptable ranges despite disturbance. It may operate through feedback, anticipation, constraints, buffering, redundancy, local autonomy or changes to the environment.

Why use this distinction?

Regulation is often collapsed into command and control. Ashby instead asks what variety of disturbance reaches the system, what responses are available and which outcomes count as acceptable. The regulator may be distributed rather than a boss or central unit.

Key distinctions

  • regulation vs domination
  • regulator vs manager
  • acceptable range vs one fixed target
  • distributed vs central regulation
  • regulation vs optimisation

Historical development

  • physiological regulation and governor analysis predate cybernetics
  • Wiener connected control and communication
  • Ashby formalised regulation in terms of variety and outcomes

Ideas it depends on

  • disturbances

  • acceptable outcomes
  • responses and constraints
  • information about relevant differences

Connections to practice

  • identify the variable that must remain viable
  • map disturbance variety before adding controls
  • ask where regulatory intelligence already sits
  • protect local discretion where it carries necessary variety

Common confusions

  • regulation means bureaucracy
  • the regulator must be central
  • tighter control always improves regulation
  • one target captures viability

Open questions

  • legal and institutional regulation as a distinct lineage
  • distributed regulation cases
