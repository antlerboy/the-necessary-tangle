Reading the CoExplorer maps

19 September 2026 · Release 0.25

A map can show an argument, a classification, a history, or a proposed mechanism. The meaning of its lines determines what can be learned from it. These three CoExplorer maps offer useful comparisons with The Necessary Tangle; two Anselm articles offer a different way into the field, through people, reading, and things to try.

Three maps, with different questions

The cybernetics map organises concepts around named thinkers. The systems map presents knowledge traditions and a genealogy. The Troncale map focuses on systems concepts and linkage propositions. They are complementary reading entrances, not interchangeable histories of one settled field.

Peter Tuddenham's CoExplorer teaching project, credited with the College of Exploration, is a key comparison source. Credit for the project does not make every interpretation an established historical or scientific claim. This page links to the original work and retains its separate identity.

The maps' displayed totals disagree across pages and sometimes within a page. The source register below records the observed numbers. No complete dataset has been imported, and none of those totals is counted as verified Tangle coverage.

Reading a relationship

The CoExplorer guide makes the kind of relation visible. The comparison below is Tangle's editorial assessment of how to preserve those distinctions. It is not an automatic translation of one graph into the other.

These distinctions can be expressed through the atlas's existing statement fields. This review adds no relationships from the external maps to the atlas. A future import would need to resolve identities and verify each claim's meaning and source, rather than convert every line by matching its label.

A proposition has a range of validity

McNamara and Troncale's 2012 paper, pp. 3-4, separates evidence for a relation in a particular system from the proposal that it applies elsewhere. Its criteria allow a directional or mutual operator between processes, with broader applicability requiring further investigation. This is a useful discipline for reading the later map.

For Tangle, the practical consequence is to keep three questions separate: what was observed, which relationship is proposed, and where that proposition has been tested. A statement about a physical system does not automatically become a warranted account of an organisation. This is an editorial application of the distinction, not an empirical finding from the paper.

The 1978 chapter's abstract and bibliographic record are available. The full chapter remains unread in this review. Its title and the later maps' counts cannot establish a reconciled list of original propositions.

People to encounter, things to try

Anselm Hook's field guide, dated 11 September 2026, asks what a thinker helps us do and whether someone else can use the resulting model, tool, or practice. Its groupings provide routes through a field, rather than evidence of historical transmission. The introduction discloses substantial AI assistance. Historical claims, omissions, and suggested connections need checking against the underlying work.

Tangle's editorial response is to keep purpose and operability visible without making either a verdict on validity. A runnable model still has assumptions and limits; a participatory practice may require situated judgement rather than a reusable program. Who can question or change the model matters alongside who can run it.

Hook's media list, dated 10 September 2026, offers a starting sequence and then separates books, recordings, and things to run. It connects study with experiment. The list has been read here; its recommended works have not thereby been reviewed or tested.

For future reading routes, this suggests recording the form of each resource, what a reader can try, any access requirements, and what has actually been checked. Tangle's practice collection is an existing route into exercises; it does not stand in for assessment of every recommendation.

Anselm Hook: A field guide to the systems thinkers (11 September 2026)

Article read through ordinary browser; individual entries unverified

The guide groups thinkers by purpose and by whether an outsider can use their model, tool, or practice. Hook states that it was largely compiled with AI assistance and suggestions from friends. It is a curated discovery aid, not primary verification of its historical or biographical claims. Its inclusion of Tangle is not independent validation of this atlas.

Checked: Introduction; organising questions; field collectors; omissions.

L. Raphael Troncale: Linkage Propositions between Fifty Principal Systems Concepts (1978)

Publisher metadata, abstract, and references read; full chapter unread

The abstract identifies fragmented terminology and the need to organise relations among systems concepts. The chapter title's 'Fifty' does not independently verify any map's numerical totals. The author-hosted full text could not be retrieved.

Checked: Abstract; chapter pp. 29-52; DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-0555-3_2.

Curt McNamara and Len Troncale: SPT II, How to Find and Map Linkage Propositions (2012)

Abstract and criteria passage read; not a full-paper review

The authors distinguish a particular observed relation from its proposed wider applicability. A proposition specifies systems processes and an operator; its direction may be one-way or mutual. They require experimental or simulation evidence in a manifest system and further work to establish the range of validity. Their paper therefore does not make every mapped proposition a universal law.

Checked: PDF pp. 3-4, What is a Linkage Proposition? Criteria for LPs.
