Feedforward: when does the warning arrive?

For preventive action, a warning must arrive early enough for someone or something to act, and that action must have an effect in the right direction. A later warning may still help limit consequences or support learning. An early signal, a model of its likely effect, an available action, and a way to see the result are different requirements.

1. A measured disturbance arrives early

A sensor detects a change while there is still enough time for a compensating action to reach the controlled variable. A feedforward path can use that measurement and a model to select an action. If the model is imperfect or another disturbance occurs, a feedback path may respond to a resulting measured deviation, subject to the controller, available action, and timing. Early information makes preventive action possible; it does not ensure the action will be accurate or sufficient.

2. A measured disturbance arrives too late

The disturbance is detected, but its effect reaches the controlled variable before the chosen action can take effect. Measuring it does not create the missing time. Advance information might help if it is actually available and reliable; otherwise action can still seek to limit or reverse the deviation after it begins. Whether the result stays within an acceptable range depends on the system and the response, not on the label 'feedforward'.

3. A disturbance is not measured

The feedforward path has no direct measurement of that disturbance to use. If the disturbance changes an output that is measured, this measured-output feedback path may respond to the resulting deviation. It cannot respond through that measurement to an effect it does not observe, and correction can be delayed, constrained, or unsuccessful. An unmeasured disturbance is not necessarily unknowable by every other route.

What has to be known?

Follow one signal through the whole sequence: when the disturbance is sensed; when its effect would arrive; what the model predicts; who or what can act; when that action takes effect; and what outcome is measured. Compare the actual outcome with the acceptable range. If a prediction is wrong, a later measurement can reveal the mismatch, but it cannot by itself guarantee that harm is reversible or that a correction will work.

These are original illustrative scenarios, not a measured intervention or a claim about organisational performance. The distinction between a warning and the time needed to act is informed by Pawlowski and colleagues' simulations of specified industrial control models. Their ideal, non-causal, and constrained cases should not be treated as one universal rule. [Pawlowski et al., 2016, §§2, 4, and Appendix A](https://doi.org/10.3390/pr4020012).
