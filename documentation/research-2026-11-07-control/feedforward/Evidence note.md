Feedforward: source evidence note

Status: bounded research candidate; human editorial acceptance pending. The existing canonical definition and all previous source and evidence records are retained. Three existing open checks remain open: I. A. Richards and Macy lineage; management-cybernetics uses; predictive control literature. These passages do not settle those broader questions.

Primary source: Andrzej Pawlowski, Carlos Rodríguez, José Luis Guzmán, Manuel Berenguel, and Sebastián Dormido, 'Measurable Disturbances Compensation: Analysis and Tuning of Feedforward Techniques for Dead-Time Processes', Processes 4(2), article 12 (2016), https://doi.org/10.3390/pr4020012. Public archive: https://psecommunity.org/wp-content/plugins/wpor/includes/file/1807/LAPSE-2018.0161-1v1.pdf. The article credits its authors and identifies CC BY 4.0, printed p 20, PDF leaf 21. Source prose below is paraphrased; the scenarios are editorial illustrations, not claims of observed service outcomes.

F1. With the first-order-plus-dead-time process models specified in §2, the authors distinguish delay from action to output and delay from disturbance to output. Their relative delay determines whether the particular ideal classical feedforward compensator is implementable. When action's delay exceeds the disturbance delay, their ideal expression requires a negative delay. This is a conclusion for the stated model and compensator, not a claim that all useful late action is impossible. §2, printed pp 3–4, PDF leaves 4–5; equations (1) and (3).

F2. The paper's appendix constructs a combined feedback and measured-disturbance feedforward scheme. In its idealised expression, a particular choice of compensator cancels the disturbance contribution, but the authors note that this choice may be non-causal or unstable. Exact cancellation in that expression depends on its assumptions; it is not a general guarantee in an actual process. Appendix A, printed pp 17–18, PDF leaves 18–19; Figure A1 and equation (A1).

F3. In one specified simulation, a predictive controller with a positive control-effort weighting did not eliminate the disturbance even though it acted in advance. In a later simulation where the ideal classical compensator was non-realizable, the authors report complete disturbance attenuation for their separate GPC + IFF (λ = 0) + F scheme, which uses future disturbance information. They say such information is not always available. Their Table 2 evaluates disturbance-response indexes over t = 9 to 20, excluding earlier setpoint-change transients; it is not a claim of exact tracking throughout the whole run. These are simulation results for particular models and settings, not field results. §4, printed pp 12–15, PDF leaves 13–16; Figures 3–4 and Table 2.

F4. Feedback can address a measured output deviation where the controller, actuator, and timing permit it. An unmeasured disturbance cannot itself supply the paper's direct measured-disturbance feedforward signal. The broader illustration that feedback may respond once its effect becomes observable is an editorial inference from the feedback path, not a separate unmeasured-disturbance experiment in this paper. §§1–2 and Appendix A, printed pp 1–4 and 17–18, PDF leaves 2–5 and 18–19.

Qualification: the article discusses plant-model mismatch and robustness, and treats saturation in a specified simulation; it does not establish that feedback always corrects prediction error, keeps outcomes within an acceptable range, or reverses harm. §§1, 3.2.2, and 4, printed pp 1, 10–11, and 15–16, PDF leaves 2, 11–12, and 16–17. Distinguish the timing of measurement from the timing of the physical effect: seeing a result late does not mean that it occurred late.

Source discrepancies: in printed p 12, equation (27) shows equal pure delays for the two paths; §2's definition would give a zero relative delay, while the sentence after equation (27) says it is negative. Equal delays remain within the paper's implementable category, but do not repeat the stated negative sign as an established result. On printed p 14 the text describes a disturbance of magnitude two at time one, while Figure 4 plots a step from zero to one at time ten. Also, printed p 13 text gives a classical controller CSE value of 0.12, while Table 1 gives 0.15. The relevant PDF page images and extracted text have been checked. No particular disputed numeric value is used in the scenarios above. These discrepancies are recorded for review, not silently corrected.
