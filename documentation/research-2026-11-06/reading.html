People can describe the same service differently and still have unequal power to change it. Follow that difficulty through four concepts, three practice labs, and a repaired connection. This is an editorial reading route, not a claim that these authors share one theory or form a historical lineage.

What does an observer select?

Start with {{atlas:concept_observer}}. Record the distinction that makes an observation possible: a waiting-list report, for example, needs a definition of a case, a start, and an end. Changing the definition can change what becomes visible. It doesn't follow that every account fits the evidence equally well, or that a situated account needs no testing.

{{atlas:concept_multiple_perspectives}} asks what changes when other accounts enter an inquiry. Different views may expose missing consequences, incompatible purposes, or different kinds of knowledge. Collecting them doesn't itself produce agreement. Ask what evidence could revise each account, and which differences must remain in the record.

An invented stress test: a service's reported wait falls because it stops counting people whose referrals were rejected. The report may be arithmetically correct while its boundary hides the people carrying the cost. Check the definition and the omitted population before celebrating the result.

Whose account can change the decision?

{{atlas:concept_boundary_critique}} carries the inquiry beyond whose description is visible. Who defines improvement, controls resources, supplies expertise, and can challenge the account? People affected by a decision may be absent from the group making it. Naming their absence isn't a substitute for giving a challenge some route into the decision.

The Critical Systems Heuristics lab keeps an actual account and an ought account separate, records unresolved disagreement, and asks what happens when the inquiry has no authority to settle the boundary. Its worked case is constructed for learning; it doesn't establish a successful client intervention.

Open the practice lab: Critical Systems Heuristics. After making the boundary judgements inspectable, test a narrower claim: which decision can this inquiry actually inform, who can change it, and who needs a way to contest it?

Who can act, and on what terms?

{{atlas:concept_structure_agency}} offers a way to examine action without treating either a person's choice or an organisation's structure as the whole explanation. Ask which rules, resources, relationships, and existing practices make an action possible; then ask how that action reproduces or changes those conditions. The developed profile locates the particular account rather than merging all theories of agency.

Continue with the Multi-methodology lab. It asks what survives a move between methods: the question, the evidence, the participants' meanings, and the unresolved dispute. A useful second method doesn't automatically validate the assumptions of the first. The Ethics and power lab then makes the next action conditional on authority, consequences, and a named reason to revise or stop.

An invented stress test: a receiving team understands every distinction in a referral but cannot authorise any response the person needs. Better communication hasn't supplied decision rights, staff time, or resources. State what can happen after the signal arrives.

Two meanings of autonomy

The connection from {{atlas:concept_autopoiesis}} to {{atlas:concept_autonomy}} now records a distinction. The biological account concerns production of the organisation constituting a unity. The managerial entry concerns operational discretion within wider constraints. A statement about a whole producing itself doesn't assign decision rights to a part. The revised connection retains its identifier and earlier provenance, with passages for each sense and an explicit editorial-comparison status.

Try Multi-methodology and Ethics and power, or follow the complete reading journey. For each transition, keep the evidence, interpretation, authority, and possible action inspectable.
