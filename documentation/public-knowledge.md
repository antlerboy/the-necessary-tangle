## Adaptation

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

Change in a system's organisation, behaviour or relations that improves or preserves its capacity to remain viable in a changing environment.

### Summary

Adaptation is relational: change is adaptive only with respect to some environment, timescale and viability condition. Local adjustment can make the larger system more fragile.

### Why it matters

Transformation programmes often demand change without specifying what environmental variety has changed or what identity must be preserved. The result is motion rather than adaptation.

### Key distinctions

- adaptation vs any change
- local fit vs whole-system viability
- reactive adjustment vs anticipatory learning
- adaptation vs optimisation

### Historical development

- biology and evolution
- cybernetics and regulation
- complex adaptive systems
- organisational learning

### Ideas it depends on

- environmental change
- variation and response
- feedback and learning
- identity or viability criterion

### What develops from it

- viability
- resilience
- learning
- strategic intelligence
- innovation

### Connections to practice

- state what changed in the environment
- track local and wider effects
- compare adaptation timescales
- retain variety and slack for unknown change

### Common confusions

- adaptation is always improvement
- survival proves good design
- optimisation increases adaptability
- change at one level helps every level

### Open questions and checks

- Ashby and ultrastability
- Beer System 4
- evolutionary adaptation
- path dependence and maladaptation

## Anthro-complexity

Type: Tradition
Public depth: profile

A body of theory and practice concerned with complexity in human systems, foregrounding meaning, intentionality, identity, narrative and the limits of treating people as interchangeable agents.

Anthro-complexity addresses complexity in human systems without treating people as simple agents analogous to insects, particles or interchangeable units. It foregrounds meaning, play, making, narrative, identity and intentionality.

## Autonomy

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

The operational freedom and regulatory capacity of a part to respond to its local environment without being reduced to a passive execution point of the whole.

### Summary

Autonomy is not independence. A viable part needs discretion, information and resources, while remaining connected to wider identity and constraints.

### Why it matters

Local variety usually outruns central knowledge. Suppressing autonomy can make the whole legible to the centre while making it less able to respond where the work happens.

### Key distinctions

- autonomy vs independence
- discretion vs abandonment
- local regulation vs central permission
- part autonomy vs whole viability

### Historical development

## Boundary

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A purpose- and observer-dependent distinction that marks what is treated as inside, outside or marginal to a system of concern and thereby shapes explanation, legitimacy and intervention.

### Summary

A boundary makes inquiry and action possible by limiting what must be handled at once. It also externalises people, effects and responsibilities. Good systems work therefore treats boundary choice as practical, political and ethical.

### Why it matters

Boundaries decide who counts, what becomes a cost, which outcomes are visible and where responsibility stops. Changing the boundary can change the apparent problem without changing any events.

### Key distinctions

- system boundary vs organisational border
- analytic boundary vs operational boundary
- inside/outside vs centre/margin
- temporary focus vs reified entity
- boundary judgement vs boundary object

### Historical development

- general systems and system/environment distinction
- critical systems heuristics
- systemic intervention and boundary critique
- boundary-spanning practice

### Ideas it depends on

- observer
- purpose
- context

### What develops from it

- intervention design

## Boundary critique

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

Boundary critique is the systematic examination of judgements about what and whom a situation, inquiry or proposal treats as relevant, including the facts, values, interests and voices those judgements admit or exclude.

### Summary

Boundary critique asks how judgements about relevance shape what counts as fact, value, improvement, expertise and legitimate participation.

### Why it matters

Every systems intervention draws a boundary. Making that boundary discussable is a condition for responsible inquiry, especially when benefits and harms fall on people who did not define the system of concern.

### Key distinctions

- boundary vs perimeter
- relevance vs existence
- involved vs affected
- fact and value as boundary-conditioned

## Bounded applicability

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

Principle that approaches have conditions under which they are useful and should not be treated as context-free universal solutions.

### Summary

Bounded applicability says that a method or explanation earns validity within specified conditions rather than by being asserted as a context-free solution.

### Why it matters

It is a useful antidote to method fundamentalism. It also imposes work: the boundary, evidence and transition conditions must be made explicit rather than invoked as a general escape clause.

### Key distinctions

- context-sensitive validity vs relativism
- boundary conditions vs disclaimer
- usefulness here vs truth everywhere

### Historical development

- systems boundary work

## Chris Mowles

## Complex responsive processes

Type: Tradition
Public depth: profile

A perspective on organising in which continuity, novelty, identity and power emerge in local interaction among interdependent people rather than being the straightforward implementation of a system-wide plan.

### Summary

Complex responsive processes treats organising as the ongoing patterning of local interaction. Broader organisational patterns constrain and enable action, but they do not operate as an external system that a manager can stand outside and control.

### Why it matters

It exposes a fault line inside systems | cybernetics | complexity: whether models of systems and self-organisation illuminate human organising or quietly reintroduce an engineer standing outside the situation.

### Key distinctions

- Local interaction and population-wide pattern are mutually constitutive.
- Power is present in ordinary interaction rather than added later as a contextual variable.
- Emergence does not imply harmony, decentralisation or benign self-organisation.

### Historical development

- Developed in the University of Hertfordshire Complexity and Management community, especially through Ralph Stacey and colleagues.
- Uses complexity sciences as resources for analogy while explicitly disputing some systems metaphors for human action.

### Ideas it depends on

- Emergence
- Self-organisation
- Interaction
- Power
- Paradox

### What develops from it

- Complexity approaches to leadership
- Reflexive management inquiry
- Practice-based accounts of change

### Connections to practice

- Attending to live interaction rather than treating plans as causes of later outcomes.
- Developing practical judgement through reflexive inquiry.
- Taking conflict, identity and power seriously in accounts of organisational change.

### Common confusions

- That local interaction means small-scale interaction is all that matters.
- That no one controls the whole means no one has more power than anyone else.

## Emergence

## Feedback

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A circular causal or informational relation in which an output or consequence affects later input, state or action. The sign, delay, reference condition and mechanism must be stated.

### Summary

Feedback is not merely receiving comments. It is a circular relation in which consequences return to influence what happens next. The loop may counteract change, reinforce it, oscillate, delay response or fail because no actor can use the returned information.

### Why it matters

Systems work often says feedback when it means reporting, evaluation, data or learning. Those are only feedback when they close a loop that can alter subsequent action. Sign, delay, reference condition and response capacity determine what the loop actually does.

### Key distinctions

- negative or balancing vs positive or reinforcing feedback
- feedback vs feedforward
- feedback signal vs capacity to respond
- physical causal loop vs returned information
- feedback mechanism vs homeostatic condition
- measurement vs learning

### Historical development

- Maxwell 1868: mathematical governor analysis as a precursor
- Black 1934: stabilised negative-feedback amplifiers
- Rosenblueth, Wiener and Bigelow 1943: purposive behaviour and negative feedback
- Wiener 1948: cybernetic synthesis across animals and machines

### Ideas it depends on

- distinguishable input, output and state
- a return path through which consequences can alter later conditions
- time, delay and loop gain
- a reference or viability condition where correction is claimed

### What develops from it

- control and regulation
- system dynamics
- management cybernetics
- learning-loop metaphors
- homeostatic and adaptive models

### Connections to practice

- ask what information returns to whom
- test whether anyone can alter action in response
- state the delay before effects become visible
- separate balancing from reinforcing loops
- look for loops that reward gaming rather than purpose

### Common confusions

- more feedback is always better
- positive means good and negative means bad
- a dashboard is feedback even when nobody can act
- homeostasis and feedback are synonyms
- all loops stabilise

### Open questions and checks

- Nyquist and Bode lineage
- Forrester and system dynamics
- Bateson, Argyris and learning
- Beer and organisational feedback channels

## Feedforward

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A control or coordination relation in which information about intended conditions or measured disturbances is used to act before the resulting output is observed; effectiveness depends on the adequacy of the model or prediction.

### Summary

Feedforward acts before the result is available to be measured. It uses intention, forecast or measured disturbance to shape action in advance. It is usually strongest when combined with feedback that corrects modelling error and surprise.

### Why it matters

Waiting for failure before responding is often costly. Feedforward can prepare capacity, prevent deviation and make intentions interpretable. Its weakness is that it depends on the quality of the model, forecast and disturbance sensing.

### Key distinctions

- anticipatory action vs reaction to output
- feedforward vs open-loop command
- forecast or model vs certainty
- feedforward information vs feedback correction

### Historical development

- anticipatory control developed alongside feedback control
- the term also developed a communication meaning in cybernetic discussions
- modern engineering commonly combines feedforward with feedback

### Ideas it depends on

- a sensed or anticipated disturbance
- a model or expectation of effects
- capacity to act before output changes

### What develops from it

- predictive control
- advance capacity planning
- briefing and expectation-setting
- combined feedforward-feedback architectures

### Connections to practice

- ask what can be known early enough to matter
- state the assumptions in the predictive model
- retain feedback for unmeasured disturbances
- distinguish preparation from premature control

### Common confusions

- feedback supplied early
- perfect prediction
- a substitute for feedback
- any plan is feedforward

### Open questions and checks

- I. A. Richards and Macy lineage
- management-cybernetics uses
- predictive control literature

## Information theory

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

The mathematical study of the quantification, coding and reliable transmission of uncertainty-bearing signals, including entropy, channel capacity, redundancy and noise.

### Summary

Information theory supplies a mathematical account of uncertainty, coding and communication through noisy channels. It is powerful precisely because it brackets semantic meaning and studies the structure and limits of transmission.

### Why it matters

Systems and cybernetics use information in several senses. Confusing Shannon information with meaning, truth, knowledge or value produces category errors. The mathematical theory can tell us how much uncertainty is reduced, not whether the message is wise, accurate or humane.

### Key distinctions

- information quantity vs semantic meaning
- signal vs message interpretation
- entropy vs disorder as a loose metaphor
- channel capacity vs organisational capacity
- data availability vs usable information

### Historical development

- Nyquist and Hartley as technical precursors
- Shannon 1948: mathematical theory of communication
- Wiener 1948: cybernetic use of information and control
- Ashby: information and variety in regulation

### Ideas it depends on

- a set of possible messages or states
- probability distribution
- source, channel, receiver and noise
- coding and decoding distinctions

### What develops from it

- coding theory
- communication engineering
- cybernetic information flows
- quantitative complexity measures

### Connections to practice

- ask what uncertainty is reduced for which decision
- separate data transmission from interpretation
- measure the channel as well as the message
- notice what the classification scheme makes invisible

### Common confusions

- information means meaning
- more data means better knowledge
- entropy is simply chaos
- a message has the same informational value to every observer

### Open questions and checks

- Hartley and Nyquist lineage
- Ashby-Shannon formal relation
- semantic information traditions

## Negative feedback

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

### Summary

## Positive feedback

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A feedback relation in which the returned effect reinforces rather than counteracts a change in the relevant variable; the sign, loop gain, delay and limiting conditions must be specified.

### Summary

Positive feedback reinforces change. It can create growth, escalation, tipping, latching, path dependence or oscillation. What stops the process is usually found in constraints, saturation, resource limits or another balancing loop.

### Why it matters

Reinforcing loops explain why small differences can become large and why systems can move rapidly away from an apparently stable condition. They are central to contagion, polarisation, bubbles, collapse, learning and mobilisation, but are not inherently beneficial or harmful.

### Key distinctions

- positive sign vs positive value
- reinforcing loop vs exponential growth without limit
- amplification vs simple correlation
- loop polarity vs outcome desirability

### Historical development

- early regenerative circuits predate the cybernetic synthesis
- mid-century control and cybernetics established positive and negative loop language
- system dynamics popularised reinforcing loop notation

### Ideas it depends on

- feedback loop
- returned effect aligned with the direction of change
- loop gain and delay
- limiting conditions

### What develops from it

- tipping and lock-in
- escalation and vicious or virtuous circles
- oscillation and instability
- path-dependent growth

### Connections to practice

- identify what reinforces what
- look for the limit that eventually stops growth
- do not label the loop virtuous or vicious before examining consequences
- test whether intervention also strengthens the loop

### Common confusions

- positive means good
- reinforcing growth continues forever
- all positive feedback is instability
- praise is the technical meaning

### Open questions and checks

- historical lineage before Black
- system dynamics source locators
- nonlinear and tipping-point literature

## Recursion

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A family of domain-specific relations in which a definition, operation, structure or organisation is reapplied to instances of the same kind. A conforming account must state the domain, recurrence rule and boundary or termination condition; organisational recursion is not automatically computational recursion or visual self-similarity.

### Summary

Recursion is a family resemblance across domains, not one universal mechanism. A definition may refer to itself through smaller cases; a computation may call itself; an organisation may contain operational systems with corresponding viability requirements.

### Why it matters

Nested diagrams easily look profound. A sound recursive claim states what recurs, what changes between levels, what the base or boundary condition is and why the same structural relation applies.

### Key distinctions

- recursive definition vs recursive computation

- recursion vs hierarchy

- organisational recursion vs mathematical recursion
- recursion vs reflexivity

## Regulation

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

### Why it matters

- responses and constraints
- information about relevant differences

### What develops from it

- good regulator theorem

## Requisite variety

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

The variety needed in a regulator, given disturbances, transformations and acceptable outcomes, to achieve the intended reduction in outcome variety. It is a limit on regulation, not a general instruction to maximise complication.

### Summary

Ashby's law sets a limit on regulation. Disturbance variety can only be reduced to the extent that the regulator has relevant, selectable and effective response variety within the transformation being regulated.

### Why it matters

The slogan is often used to justify more choice, more management or decentralisation in the abstract. The law asks a harder question: which responses can distinguish and counter which disturbances, under what communication and resource constraints?

### Key distinctions

- variety vs vague complexity
- available response vs nominal option
- attenuation and amplification vs maximal complication
- law of requisite variety vs good regulator theorem
- necessary regulatory capacity vs a universal organisational prescription

### Historical development

- Ashby development in the early 1950s
- An Introduction to Cybernetics, 1956
- Conant and Ashby 1970: good regulator theorem
- Beer: application in management cybernetics and VSM

### Ideas it depends on

- variety
- regulation
- disturbance set
- response set
- transformation between actions and outcomes
- acceptable outcomes

### What develops from it

- variety engineering
- Viable System Model
- autonomy and decentralisation arguments
- design of information channels

### Connections to practice

- map where variety is generated, attenuated and amplified
- increase response repertoire only where it can affect outcomes
- reduce avoidable disturbance rather than merely adding control
- protect local autonomy where it carries relevant knowledge

### Common confusions

- the controller must simply be more complicated
- more choice is always better
- the theorem proves decentralisation in every case
- the good regulator theorem is the same theorem

### Open questions and checks

- exact provenance of absorb variety wording
- Beer primary application pages
- empirical organisational applications

## Self-organisation

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A process in which interactions among components generate or maintain an organised macro-pattern without a central external controller prescribing that pattern; the system boundary, local rules, constraints and environment must be specified.

### Summary

Self-organisation describes organised macro-patterns arising through interactions among components rather than being prescribed by an external central controller. The system still depends on boundaries, local rules, resources, constraints and an environment.

### Why it matters

The phrase is often instrumentalised as if leaders can order emergence or simply withdraw. A useful account specifies the local interactions, constraints and selection processes that generate the pattern, and asks who drew the system boundary.

### Key distinctions

- no central prescription vs no causes or constraints
- self-organisation vs emergence
- local rules vs individual intention
- open system vs isolated system
- description by an observer vs intrinsic organisation

### Historical development

- Ashby 1962: observer-dependent analysis of self-organisation
- von Foerster 1960: systems and environments, order from noise
- later complexity science: local interaction and global pattern

### Ideas it depends on

- interacting components
- local rules or couplings
- energy and resource flows
- constraints and boundary
- time and adaptation

### What develops from it

- distributed coordination
- swarm and network models
- adaptive organisation
- autopoiesis and enaction debates

### Connections to practice

- specify what interactions are being enabled or blocked
- look for power embedded in rules and resources
- do not treat leaderless as ungoverned
- intervene in conditions rather than promise a designed outcome

### Common confusions

- no management is required
- the system organises itself towards good ends
- emergence and self-organisation are synonyms
- constraints prevent self-organisation

### Open questions and checks

- Prigogine and dissipative structures
- autopoiesis
- agent-based modelling lineage
- social power and institutional constraints

## Variety

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

The number of distinguishable elements or possible states in a specified set, sometimes expressed logarithmically; the distinctions and observer must be stated.

### Summary

Variety counts distinguishable possibilities. A system may have many physical states but little usable variety if the observer cannot distinguish them or the regulator cannot select among effective responses.

### Why it matters

Variety turns vague talk about complexity into a question about differences that matter for regulation. It also makes the observer visible: what counts as a different state depends on purpose, sensing and classification.

### Key distinctions

- physical states vs distinguishable states
- nominal options vs usable responses
- variety vs diversity as a value
- variety vs semantic richness
- count vs logarithmic measure

### Historical development

- Ashby developed variety as a central cybernetic measure
- information theory provided related logarithmic tools
- management cybernetics applied attenuation and amplification of variety

### Ideas it depends on

- a specified set
- criteria of distinction
- an observer or measuring apparatus
- a purpose or regulatory question

### What develops from it

- law of requisite variety
- variety engineering
- attenuation and amplification
- regulatory capacity analysis

### Connections to practice

- state whose distinctions count
- separate the variety seen by citizens, frontline workers and management
- ask which options can actually change outcomes
- do not add reporting variety that no one can absorb

### Common confusions

- variety is objective and observer-free
- more variety is always better
- variety means diversity or complexity in general
- every available option is usable

### Open questions and checks

- formal relation to Shannon entropy
- later variety engineering methods

### Sources

- An Introduction to Cybernetics — https://ashby.info/bibliography.html
- A Mathematical Theory of Communication — https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x

## Variety amplification

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A deliberate increase in effective sensing or response variety available to a regulator, channel or operational unit.

### Summary

Amplification expands what a system can notice or do through capability, discretion, tools, coordination, networks and access to resources.

### Why it matters

When the environment cannot responsibly be simplified, the system must improve its repertoire. Giving people more cases without more authority or capability is workload amplification, not regulatory amplification.

### Key distinctions

## Variety attenuation

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

A deliberate reduction in effective variety so that relevant disturbances, signals or choices become tractable without pretending the discarded variety never existed.

### Summary

Attenuation protects a regulator from being overwhelmed. It can simplify, buffer, aggregate or redesign demand. It can also erase inconvenient people and conditions if the boundary and purpose are wrong.

### Why it matters

Forms, triage, standards, thresholds and dashboards are attenuation devices. Their value depends on whether they preserve the differences needed for responsible action.

- standardisation vs loss of requisite detail

## Viability

Type: Concept
Public depth: profile

The capacity of a system to continue as a recognisable whole by balancing present operations, regulation, learning, adaptation and identity under changing conditions.

### Summary

Viability is not short-term survival or financial solvency. It concerns whether a system can keep producing itself as a coherent actor while its environment and internal conditions change.

### Why it matters

A viable organisation must handle today's work, disturbances and accountability while sensing and preparing for tomorrow. Central control cannot substitute for local regulatory capacity.

### Key distinctions

- viability vs short-term survival
- viability vs efficiency
- viability vs comparative evolutionary fitness
- identity continuity vs structural fixity
- present regulation vs future adaptation
- whole viability vs local optimisation

### Historical development

- later organisational applications

### Ideas it depends on

## Viable System Model (VSM)

Type: Method or methodology
Public depth: profile

A management-cybernetic model for diagnosing and designing the functions and communications by which an organisation maintains viable autonomy and cohesion in a changing environment across recursive levels.

### Summary

The VSM is a model of the functions and communications needed for an organisation to remain viable in a changing environment. It distinguishes autonomous operations from meta-systemic functions for coordination, internal regulation, intelligence and policy, and applies recursively.

### Why it matters

The model makes the autonomy-cohesion problem explicit. It asks whether local operations can absorb relevant variety, whether conflicts are damped, whether the whole gains synergy, whether intelligence reaches strategy and whether identity closes the system without suffocating it.

### Key distinctions

- function vs department
- model vs organisation chart
- system-in-focus vs whole legal organisation
- autonomy vs independence
- System 3 control vs System 4 intelligence
- recursion vs hierarchy
- diagnosis vs prescriptive blueprint

### Historical development

- Beer developed the model from management practice and cybernetics
- Brain of the Firm and Heart of Enterprise developed the theory
- Diagnosing the System for Organizations provided a diagnostic guide
- Cybersyn and later organisational practice extended its application

### Ideas it depends on

- regulation
- requisite variety
- feedback and information channels
- organisational recursion
- autonomy and cohesion
- system-environment distinction

### What develops from it

- VSM diagnosis and design
- variety engineering
- recursive governance
- Team Syntegrity as a related Beer practice

### Connections to practice

- start from primary operations and their environments
- map actual regulatory work rather than formal boxes
- look for missing or overloaded functions
- test communication channels for timeliness and capacity
- keep diagnosis provisional and observer-aware

### Common confusions

- five boxes to assign to departments
- a universal ideal structure
- central control dressed as systems thinking
- recursion is just more hierarchy
- System 5 is the chief executive
- governance is from outside the system

### Open questions and checks

- primary page locators for recursion and requisite variety
- critical and empirical evaluations
- relations to power, ethics and democratic governance
