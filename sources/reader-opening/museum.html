The museum alarm

A museum keeps a collection in a temperature-controlled store. In this example, we assume the store has a suitable temperature range, a working sensor, and equipment that can both heat and cool. The duty officer works in another building. Her phone lights up with the words ‘outside range’.

What the officer knows when the alarm rings
Store temperatureMessageAction needed
Below rangeOutside rangeSwitch on the heating
Above rangeOutside rangeSwitch on the cooling

A louder alarm would get her attention. It would still leave her guessing which switch to press. A message saying ‘too hot’ or ‘too cold’ gives her enough information for this decision. The same message twice is a failure only because the two situations call for different actions.

What can the building absorb?

W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety relates disturbances, the responses available, and the outcomes they can produce. A brief spell of hot weather may leave the store temperature unchanged because the walls are well insulated. The officer can ignore that change outdoors. What counts for the collection is the variation that gets through the walls and calls for a response.

W. Ross Ashby, An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956), chapter 11, §§11/5–11/11. Section 11/9 deals with a transformation that already restricts the possible outcomes. Requisite variety in the atlas.

Where is the model?

Once the message distinguishes hot from cold, the officer can use a simple rule. Several high readings all lead to cooling. Several low readings all lead to heating. Her choices preserve the distinction that matters for operating the switch. She could embody that rule in a thermostat without ever drawing a picture of the store.

In their 1970 paper, Roger Conant and Ashby specify a system, its possible events and their probabilities, a regulator, and a way of scoring the outcomes. They show that in a simplest regulator that minimises the entropy of the outcomes, each system event maps to a particular regulatory action. Several events may map to the same action. The museum’s conservation staff must still decide which outcomes are acceptable. A store that always ends up too hot is perfectly predictable.

Roger C. Conant and W. Ross Ashby, ‘Every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system’ (1970), theorem and proof, §5. The good regulator theorem in the atlas.

Who gets the message in time?

Stafford Beer distinguishes the route a signal travels from the device or convention that converts it at a boundary. Here the sensor turns a temperature into a reading and the alarm software turns the reading into a phone message. ‘Outside range’ discards information at the second conversion. A phone that delivers the correct message after the officer’s shift has a different fault. And if only the facilities manager holds the controls, replacing the sensor will leave the officer just as stuck.

Stafford Beer, ‘The Viable System Model’ (1989, electronic edition 2002), pp. 6–8 and Appendix 2, p. 16. Beer’s second organisational principle concerns the capacity of a channel over time. His third concerns the distinctions a transducer can carry across a boundary. Transduction in the atlas.
