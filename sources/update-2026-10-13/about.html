About The Necessary Tangle

The Necessary Tangle helps you explore ideas, the people who developed them, and how they're used. You can look up a term, follow a short reading route, or inspect the connections on a map.

What you'll find here

This is a growing reference atlas of systems | cybernetics | complexity: overlapping fields concerned with relationships, organisation, feedback, change and the limits of what we can know. It brings together concepts, people, methods, books, institutions and examples of practice.

The aim is to help you understand an idea in context. Who developed it? What does it depend on? How has it been used, and where do people disagree? You don't need to accept one school of thought to explore the others.

How to use the atlas, with an example

What a connection means

Every line has a stated meaning. 'A wrote B', 'A taught B', 'A criticised B' and 'A is needed to explain B' are different relationships. A shared word, a nearby position on the map, or an earlier date doesn't establish influence.

For example, a book can explain feedback without establishing who first developed the idea. A later author can cite that book without agreeing with it. Select a connection to read its wording, direction, sources and status.

How to read sources and uncertainty

Why the name?

These fields don't form a neat family tree. Ideas cross between disciplines, people learn through several traditions, and disagreements matter. The 'tangle' keeps those overlaps visible. 'Necessary' asks what you need to understand an idea or justify a connection; it doesn't declare every item essential.

Benjamin P Taylor began the project after years of collecting and questioning maps of the field, and contributing to SCiO's Systems Thinking Body of Knowledge. David Ing's suggestion to map the influences around practitioners helped shape its attention to people as well as concepts.

How much can you rely on it?

This is a public alpha: useful material alongside gaps and unfinished work. A developed entry has received more research than a brief entry, but that doesn't make it a final authority. Candidate and disputed statements remain labelled. Check the cited source when the distinction matters to your work.

What shapes the coverage?

Coverage reflects the sources available, the languages read and the curator's choices. Feedback, viability, boundaries and systems practice have received substantial attention. Human and institutional histories and many parts of complexity still need deeper research. A sparse area doesn't mean it's unimportant.

Who is responsible?

Curated by Benjamin P Taylor. The atlas draws on the work of many authors, practitioners, communities and map makers. AI assists with research, organisation, coding and drafting; it can also introduce errors. Benjamin is responsible for the editorial choices. Automated checks test consistency and operation, not the truth of every statement.

Help improve it, or reuse it

Corrections, missing sources and competing interpretations are welcome. Use the contribution route to prepare an email or a public issue; you don't need a GitHub account to email Benjamin. Give the entry, what should change, and the evidence. Contributions are reviewed before inclusion.

The precise meaning of a line

'Explicit semantics' is the technical term for saying exactly what a connection means. The atlas records its two ends, direction, relationship, wording, scope, evidence and review status so that readers can inspect the claim.

What enters the public site

Public pages use public sources or identifiable bibliographic references. Private research can suggest leads, but doesn't grant permission to publish a document or establish its claims. Rights, attribution and source restrictions still apply.
